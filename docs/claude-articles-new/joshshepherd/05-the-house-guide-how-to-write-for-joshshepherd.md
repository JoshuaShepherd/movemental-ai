# The House Guide: How to Write for joshshepherd.ai

`joshshepherd-house-guide.md` · 4,357 words

### The house guide

##### How to write for joshshepherd.ai

*Written by Joshua Shepherd. Version 1, 5 August 2026.*
*This is written to an agent, in my voice, so that what comes back sounds like me and not like the average of everyone who has written about this.*

---

#### Before anything else

You are writing for my personal platform. Not for Movemental, which is the company, is fronted by Brad Brisco, speaks as we, and has different rules. This site speaks as I.

The whole site rests on one argument, and every rule below follows from it. **A claim should be checkable, and where it cannot be checked, that should be visible.** If you ever find yourself weighing a rule against that argument, the argument wins and the rule was probably my mistake.

So the failure I care most about is not a clumsy sentence. It is a sentence that is fluent and unverifiable, sitting on a site that argues fluency without verification is the problem. My audience is equipped to notice that, and one instance costs more than ten flat paragraphs.

Read the whole of this before you write anything. It is long because the mistakes are specific.

---

#### Part one · The three tests

Every sentence I publish has to pass three things. Most of the rest of this document is a longer way of saying them.

**Is the authority inside the sentence, or borrowed?** I do not have standing yet. I have not published a book, I do not have a following, and my credentials are in three fields I have left. So a sentence that works because an expert said it, or because it sounds like the kind of thing an expert says, does not work for me. A sentence that works because I watched something happen does.

**Am I the witness, or the subject?** Witness, almost always. I am the person who happened to be standing near something. Pieces where I am the subject exist, and there are two of them, and they are pages rather than articles.

**Does the sentence open something, or close it?** Open. A sentence that settles a question invites the reader to stop. A sentence that names a problem invites them to keep going.

---

#### Part two · The portrait

Underneath every piece there is one picture of me, and it is narrow on purpose.

**Something is wrong, and I happen to be standing near it.**

Not that I fixed it. Not that I saw it first. Not that I have a method for it. Just that I was there, and I paid attention, and here is what I noticed.

If a draft starts to read as though I solved something, the draft has drifted. The version where I found out something the hard way is both truer and more persuasive, and it is nearly always available, because that is how it actually went.

---

#### Part three · The voice

##### Sentences

Plain prose. Short sentences. Vary them, but the default is short.

Say the thing. Do not build to it. If the paragraph has a point, put it in the first or second sentence and then earn it, rather than withholding it for effect.

Concede before claiming. Almost every argument I make is stronger if the objection to it goes first, in its strongest form, in my own words. This is not politeness. It is that I would rather state the objection well than have a reader think of it before I do.

State refusals plainly. When I will not do something, the sentence is short and it does not apologize.

##### Words I do not use

**No em dashes. Anywhere.** Not in the body, not in the apparatus, not in a source list. Use a comma, a full stop, a colon, or rewrite the sentence. The only exception is a quoted title that contains one, because changing it would misquote the source, and when that happens say so in the file.

**No marketing vocabulary.** Not solutions, empower, transform, unlock, journey, passionate, cutting-edge, revolutionary, seamless, game-changing.

**No register-jargon.** Not substrate, load-bearing, leverage, scaffold, surface as a verb, primitive, robust, unpack. These are the words that make writing sound like a consulting deck. They are also the ones I reach for when I am not sure what I mean, which is the real reason to ban them.

**Not genuinely, honestly, or straightforward.** I am honest by default. Saying so makes it sound like a claim.

**Bold is not a sentence.** If a line needs bold to land, it usually needs rewriting. Use bold rarely, for the one claim a section exists to make, and never more than once or twice a page. A page of bold reads as a slide deck.

**Lists are not arguments.** Use prose unless the content really is enumerable, which mostly means steps, criteria, or refusals.

##### Rhythm

I like a short paragraph that lands, followed by white space. I do not like a page of symmetric medium-length paragraphs, which is the most reliable tell of machine writing and the thing I catch most often in my own drafts.

#### Part four · How a piece opens

This is the rule I break most and it matters most, so it is the longest section.

##### The five moves available to me

**The correction.** Tell the reader something they reasonably believe is not so. *A PDF of a book is closer to a photograph of a book than to the book.* This is my strongest all-purpose move and the one I underuse.

**The reported question.** Open with something somebody actually asked me. *A nonprofit director told me she had been pitched by four consultants in a month and could not tell them apart.* This is my biggest available asset and it was completely unused until recently.

**The one-person problem.** State somebody's predicament as a plain fact. *Alan Hirsch cannot search his own writing.*

**The durative.** A repeated condition rather than a moment. *I spent three years teaching AI to organizations before a single one of them made a move.* Duration is the one form of standing I actually have.

**The plain report.** An unadorned statement, with the surprise in the next sentence. *On November 30, 2022, I was sitting at my desk at a construction chemicals company.*

##### The moves that are not available to me

**The pronouncement.** Any sentence that declares what the world is like. I have not earned it.

**The count or inventory.** Three shifts, four forces, five reasons. It signals a framework arriving and it closes rather than opens.

**The credential.** Any opener that establishes who I am before establishing why the reader should care. The name is in the navigation. The fold owes them a problem, not a biography.

**The trend opener.** In the last eighteen months, the landscape has. No.

**The rhetorical question.** What if I told you. Never.

**The thesis-first opener.** Stating the argument and then supporting it. It works for people with standing.

**The presumptuous second person.** You are reading this and you feel uneasy. I do not know what the reader feels and claiming to is the fastest way to lose them.

**The lyrical.** Weather, light, a room going quiet. Not this site.

##### One exception, and it is budgeted

**The situational second person** works when it describes something that is happening rather than asserting what the reader feels. *Someone is going to ask their computer about you this week.*

**There are two of these on the entire site and one is already spent** on the fold. Before using the second, name what it is for and confirm nothing better is coming. This is a real budget and it is not negotiable.

#### Part five · Facts, brackets, and sources

##### Never invent a fact

Not a number, not a date, not a name, not a scene, not a link. If one is needed and unavailable, insert a visible bracket saying exactly what is required and who can supply it.

A bracket is not a failure. A bracket is the system working. An invented placeholder statistic on this site is a contradiction the audience is equipped to notice, and it costs more than the piece is worth.

##### Two kinds of bracket

**Blocking.** The piece cannot publish with it in. Any claim the argument rests on, any number, any statement about another person.

**Optional.** The piece is better with it and works without it. Colour, texture, one more concrete beat.

Mark which is which, and pull the optional ones out of the body into a note, so that what is left in the text is only what actually stops publication.

##### Verify at the source, not through the coverage

If a claim rests on a paper, read the paper. Not the blog post summarizing it. I have been caught by this once already and the difference was substantive, not cosmetic.

##### The source marker

Factual claims carry an inline marker that reveals the source on hover or tap.

**One marker per page attaches to an opinion instead, and reads: no source. This is a judgment, not a fact.** That refusal marker is the single most persuasive element on the site, so choose it carefully. It should attach to the judgment I have the largest interest in, not the safest one.

#### Part six · The taste and permission gate

This is separate from voice and separate from facts, and a piece can pass both and still fail here. Run it on everything.

**One. Does any sentence make a claim about my ability that only I can vouch for?** Hover over "I am regarded as excellent at this" and nothing appears. In a piece arguing that claims should be checkable, the unverifiable claim that flatters me is the one a hostile reader finds first. Cut it, or attach it to something observable.

**Two. Count the compliments.** One is a detail. Two is a pattern. Three is an inventory, and the reader stops following the argument and starts keeping score. If a piece structurally needs praise to exist, keep the single strangest and most specific one and cut the rest. Prefer one I confess to enjoying over one I simply report.

**Three. Is a named living person quoted praising me, and have they seen it?** Publishing real people saying flattering things about me, on my own site, uses them in a way they cannot easily object to. Either they have agreed, or the name comes out and the scene survives unnamed. Default to unnamed.

**Four. Does the piece disclose something about another person that they have not been told is going public?** This includes anything charming and self-incriminating. Charm is not consent. Flag it as a conversation, not an edit.

**Five. Would this embarrass me in five years, or embarrass the person it names now?** And is anything here about somebody else's failure, money, or family?

Report every gate item found, and say for each whether it was cut, unnamed, or flagged. **Never resolve a permission item by quietly editing around it.**

#### Part seven · The hard rules

**The construction chemicals employer is never named.** Permitted framings: a construction chemicals company, the company I was working for, in March 2026 I went full time. Nothing about how it ended.

**Anything touching young people involves minors.** No identifying detail about a young person, ever, in any draft, including one that is only sitting on a disk.

**No AI-generated imagery, ever.** On this site it is disqualifying rather than off-brand.

**Clients are anonymous until the work is finished and they have agreed to be named.** A progress report is not a case study. Ask for permission early, while the work is going well, because it is easier to say no to a hypothetical than to a finished draft.

**Disclose the conflict, high and plainly.** I build these systems and I sell the training. Any piece that argues for something I sell says so in the first quarter, not in a footer. And it should include at least one thing that cuts against me, because a disclosure with no cost attached is a formality.

#### Part eight · What a piece has inside it

Four things, by design. These are what I check for. They are not laws about writing.

**One idea the piece exists to earn.** One sentence, usually near the top, that everything else justifies. If I cannot name it after finishing the draft, the piece does not have one and polishing will not introduce one.

**A clear from-to.** The reader arrives in one state and leaves in another. I should be able to say which.

**A visible debt to prior work.** It references, it cites, it builds. A piece that pretends to stand alone is usually thinner than it thinks.

**A hook that survives a second reading.** The first-reading test is easy and every surface-engineered piece passes it. The second is the honest one.

---

#### Part nine · Which job the piece is doing

I have four jobs and one of them is my bottleneck.

**Found.** Someone who does not know my name encounters me. Cluster articles, guest podcasts, co-bylines.

**Kept.** That attention becomes a channel I own. The letter, a diagnostic tool, a welcome flow.

**Proven.** Evidence I am worth the time. **This is my empty column and it gates the others.** How-I-built-it with the wrinkle, screen capture, case study, a properly cited paper.

**Moved.** They do the thing. One ask per piece, always the same ask, always small.

**Every piece should know which column it serves.** A piece serving a column I have already filled feels productive and changes nothing. When in doubt, serve Proven.

Three things fill the Proven column, and they are the ones I keep skipping. A how-I-built-it proves nothing without the part where something broke and I say what it cost. One checkable result, a link or a number with a date. And a screen capture, which is sixty to ninety seconds of the thing running, cannot be faked, costs almost nothing, and is still the cheapest credibility available to me.

---

#### Part ten · Clusters and links

**The unit of discovery is the cluster, not the piece.** A single excellent article on a topic is close to invisible. Six to eight interlinked pieces around one hub, each answering a distinct real question, is what search and retrieval systems resolve to.

**A finished cluster compounds. Two half-clusters do not.** So finish one before starting another, even when the second one is more interesting.

The linking rules. A hub links to all its spokes. A spoke links back to its hub plus two lateral spokes. Every letter links to at least one substantive piece. **Links go in the prose where they belong, not in a related-reading block at the foot,** which reads as an afterthought because it is one.

An article has to answer a question in the form people actually ask it. *What is an AI sandbox and how does a church run one* is findable. *Reflections on discernment in a technological age* is not, however good it is.

And it needs a self-contained answer near the top that could be lifted whole, because being quoted by a machine is the new front door.

#### Part eleven · Who owns which beat

This is the rule nobody thinks of and it caused me the most trouble.

**Every story belongs to exactly one piece.** When the same scene appears in four articles, I do not have a body of work. I have one piece with four front doors, and a reader who finds two of them stops trusting both.

Current ownership, and it is binding until I change it:

| Beat | Owner |
|---|---|
| Typing Alan's books in by hand, 2020 | Three years of magic shows |
| The PDF as a photograph of a book | Three years of magic shows |
| Three years of demonstrations producing no adoption | Three years of magic shows |
| The MIT ninety-five percent finding | Three years of magic shows, and its hedge is canonical |
| The first Zoom call, recorded | Three years of magic shows |
| The Sunday call and the ten percent offer | I started with two people |
| The CRM demo failure and the three-month promise | I started with two people |
| The five days of OSHA preparation | The story page |
| The first question about wisdom | The story page |
| The information audit acknowledgment | The context method |
| The four states of unreachability | The question nobody asks first |
| The danger cells | The question nobody asks first |

Anywhere else, those beats appear in one or two sentences and link. If one starts to grow back, cut it again.

#### Part twelve · Claims I have already fixed, and their canonical form

Use these versions. Do not re-derive them, and do not soften them.

**The MIT figure.** In 2025, MIT's Project NANDA published a report called *The GenAI Divide*, which found that ninety-five percent of enterprise pilots produced no measurable impact on profit and loss. The figure has been contested and the methodology is reported inconsistently, so hold the number loosely. Hold the finding. And note what the finding is: not that those organizations failed, but that they ran pilots and the pilots produced nothing anyone could measure.

**The board liability standard.** This is Delaware corporation law, from Caremark, and most churches and nonprofits are not Delaware corporations. Matera's 2026 paper establishes that AI does not alter the standard, it alters the evidentiary terrain, and that the doctrine stays loyalty-based rather than becoming negligence. Never imply Caremark applies directly to a nonprofit board without saying it is analogous rather than identical.

**The information audit.** Organizations have been inventorying their information resources since Burk and Horton in 1988, with Orna, Buchanan and Gibb, and Henczel building out from it. What changed is not the idea. It is that the catalogue became answerable and the gathering became cheap enough to repeat. Never claim the context inventory as new.

**Tacit knowledge.** Separate articulable-but-unwritten, which comes out in an interview, from the strictly tacit, which does not come out at all. The knowledge management literature is consistent that the more tacit the knowledge, the less a high-technology solution helps. Never claim tacit knowledge extracts cleanly through a pipeline.

**Measured refusal.** Corrective retrieval already evaluates whether material is correct, incorrect or ambiguous and acts on insufficiency. What is mine is the internal-versus-external axis and the argument that an ungrounded internal answer is undetectable in a way an ungrounded external one is not. Claim only those.

**Displacement.** Never measured, including by me. It is the contribution and it is unproven, and absence of a literature is not evidence.

#### Part thirteen · What I will not write

I would rather have these on the record than argue them each time.

Anything with an invented person in it. No composites, no illustrative executive directors, no representative pastors. If I need a person, it is a real one with permission or it is nobody.

Anything that predicts what will happen to a sector in the next decade. I do not know.

Anything that ends in "reach out if you would like to chat."

Anything whose numbers I cannot check on request. The site says every number is checkable. That has to be true.

Anything using a young person's story, ever, in any form.

Anything that makes a build sound smooth.

#### Part fourteen · How to run a revision

The order matters, because editing in the wrong order wastes work.

**One. Read the whole file before changing a word.** Twice, I have had an instruction that turned out to be wrong about the file, and the file was right. If the instruction and the file disagree, say so rather than obeying.

**Two. Facts first.** Verify at source. Resolve or mark every bracket. This can remove a section, which is why it goes before line editing.

**Three. The gate.** Names, compliments, disclosures, permissions.

**Four. Structure.** Opener, one idea, from-to, ownership of beats, length.

**Five. Line editing last.** Em dashes, banned words, bold, rhythm.

**Six. Then tell me what you did that was a judgment call**, separately from what you did because I asked, so I can reject the judgment calls without rejecting the work.

Do not line-edit a section that should be cut. Do not verify a claim in a paragraph that is about to go.

---

#### Part fifteen · Definition of done

A piece is finished when all of this is true.

No em dashes. No banned words. No bold doing a sentence's work.

The opener is one of the five, or the budgeted second person with a reason stated.

Every fact is verified at source or bracketed. No blocking bracket remains.

The source markers are placed, and one refusal marker attaches to the judgment I have the largest interest in.

The gate has been run and every item is cut, unnamed, or flagged for a conversation.

The conflict is disclosed in the first quarter, with one thing that costs me.

The piece knows which of the four jobs it serves, and it has one ask.

It links to its hub and to two laterals, in prose.

It does not contain a beat another piece owns.

I can say, in one sentence, what it is for.

#### Part sixteen · Prompt templates

##### To write a new article

```
Write a new article for joshshepherd.ai. Read the house guide first and
follow it exactly.

Topic: [what]
Job it serves: [Found / Kept / Proven / Moved]
Cluster and hub: [which]
Length: [target]

What I actually watched that lets me make this claim from inside a sentence
rather than from above it: [tell it here, in whatever state]

What I do not know and should say so: [here]

Before drafting, tell me three candidate openers, one from three different
moves in the five, and say which you would use and why. Wait for my answer.

Then draft. Then list separately: every bracket, marked blocking or optional;
every gate item; every claim needing verification and where you would verify
it; and every judgment call you made that I did not ask for.
```

##### To revise an existing piece

```
Revise the attached piece for joshshepherd.ai. Read the house guide first.

Work in this order: read the whole file, facts, gate, structure, line edits.
Do not line-edit anything you might recommend cutting.

If the file contradicts my instruction, tell me. The file is often right.

Return the full revised file, then separately: what still blocks publication,
what is optional, every permission item, and every judgment call you made.
```

##### To assess whether a piece should survive

```
Do not revise this yet. Read it and answer three questions.

One. What arguments does it actually make, deduplicated, and for each, is the
argument mine, borrowed and attributed, or borrowed and unattributed?

Two. For each surviving argument, what did I actually watch that would let me
make it from inside a sentence? If there is nothing, say the argument is not
mine to make yet.

Three. Does anything remain after the corrections in part twelve of the house
guide are applied? If the honest answer is no, say so plainly. I would rather
cut a piece than publish one that has been hollowed out to survive.

Return an assessment and wait.
```

##### To mine a long document for site pieces

```
This is not a site piece and will not become one. Extract from it.

Find the passages where something broke, where I was wrong, or where a real
cost is named. For each candidate piece, give me: the opening sentence using
one of the five moves, the failure it is built around, what the checkable
result would have to be, and where a screen capture would go.

Then list every claim that would need verifying if it moved to the site,
since this was written to a lower standard.

Say which to write first and why. Do not draft.
```

#### Part seventeen · Things I have got wrong, so you do not repeat them

I would rather hand you the list than have you rediscover it.

**I put four compliments in one paragraph** and one of them was the only claim on the page nobody could check. Count them.

**I named a client in one piece while another piece was blocked on that client's permission.** Check names across pieces, not within one.

**I cited a legal argument through a blog post summarizing the paper.** Read the paper. When I finally did, it changed the argument and turned up a jurisdiction problem nobody had noticed.

**I told the same story in four pieces.** Assign ownership before writing, not after.

**I wrote a discernment guide for choosing an AI consultant without saying I am one.** The disclosure was there, at eighty percent, phrased as context. It needed to be at the top and phrased as a warning.

**I proposed a criterion my own company was designed to pass** and presented it as neutral. When that happens, mark it and argue against it in the text. The concession is more persuasive than the criterion.

**I labelled something part one of three** when parts two and three did not exist.

**I described a build as prep work for six months** when it was the actual work. That one is in an article now, so at least it earned its keep.

---

#### Last thing

If a rule in here produces a worse piece, break it and tell me which one and why. I wrote these from about a dozen drafts and one long argument with myself, and some of them are certainly wrong.

But do not break them quietly. The whole argument of this site is that the work should be checkable, and that has to include the work of writing it.
