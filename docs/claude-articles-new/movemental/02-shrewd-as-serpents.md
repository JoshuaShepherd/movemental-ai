# Shrewd as Serpents: The Dual Command for Church Leaders Facing AI

And notice where it is said. Sheep among wolves. This is not general life counsel. It is what you tell people walking into danger. The reason for shrewdness is that the situation is dangerous. Naivety in a dangerous situation is not innocence. It is negligence that has learned to describe itself as innocence.

Both halves are commanded. Shrewd without unmixed is the serpent in the garden. Unmixed without shrewd is a sheep in a wolf pack. We have spent a long time treating the second one as the safe error. It is not. It is just the more respectable one.

#### The buried talent

The parable that will not leave the fearful alone is the one about the talents, and specifically the third servant.

He does not steal. He does not squander. He buries what he was given and returns it intact, and he gives his reason plainly: he was afraid. He knew the master to be a hard man, so he protected what he had.

The verdict is severe, and it is worth sitting with why.

First, his fear is named as the cause, and it is not accepted as a defense. It is treated as the offense itself.

Second, and this is the part I find most convicting, the fear had produced a theology to justify itself. "I knew you to be a hard man." He had an account of the master that made his paralysis reasonable. That is almost always how this works. Inaction rarely arrives announcing itself as cowardice. It arrives as a considered position about the nature of the risk, and it is usually articulate.

Third, the master's response closes the last exit. At minimum, he says, you could have put it with the bankers. Even the most cautious available option involved handing it to somebody else and letting it work. Burial was not the low-risk choice. It was the only guaranteed loss.

I want to be careful here, because this parable has been used to bless a great deal of ordinary ambition by dressing it in Scripture, and church leaders are right to be wary of that. I am not saying God will judge your technology decisions. I am saying the parable makes a specific claim about the structure of fear, and that claim is portable: **doing nothing is not the neutral position, and sincerity about your reasons does not make them true.**

If your organization has decided to wait, the question worth asking is not whether the decision is defensible. It probably is. The question is whether it is a decision at all, or a fear that has found language.

#### The oil that could not be lent

Then there are the ten young women waiting for a wedding party that is late.

Five are called wise and five foolish, and the difference between them is one thing. The wise brought extra oil against a delay they could not predict. It is worth noticing that all ten fall asleep. The wise are not more vigilant. They are better provisioned.

The line that lands hardest comes at midnight, when the foolish ask to borrow. The answer is no, and it is not cruelty. There will not be enough for both. Go and buy your own.

That refusal is a statement about what kind of thing oil is. **Some capacities cannot be transferred at the moment of crisis, and cannot be acquired at the moment they are needed.**

This is the part of the current conversation that I think church leaders most consistently get wrong, and I say that having watched it up close for three years.

The assumption is that when this technology matters enough, the organization will learn it then. There will be a workshop. Somebody will figure it out. We will catch up when catching up becomes necessary.

Formation does not work that way. It never has, in any domain your church already understands. You cannot disciple someone at the moment they face the crisis. You cannot build a culture of discernment during the week you need discernment. The reason your church invests years in people before anything visible happens is that you already know this about every other kind of formation. It is equally true here.

The capacity to use a powerful tool wisely is built slowly, in low-stakes conditions, before the stakes arrive. When it arrives first and the capacity is missing, you do not get a slower version of the same outcome. You get a capable instrument in the hands of an organization with no formed judgment about it, which is worse than having nothing at all, because now the mistakes are fast and confident and they carry your name.

#### Reading the moment

There is a rebuke in Luke 12 that I find harder than the others because it is aimed at competent people.

Jesus tells the crowd they can look at the sky and tell you what the weather is doing, and then asks why they cannot interpret the present moment. And then, sharper: why do you not judge for yourselves what is right.

The charge is not ignorance. These are people who read their world expertly in ordinary matters. The charge is a selective competence, sharp about everything except the thing that carries the most consequence.

And the second sentence is the one I would put in front of every leader I know. Judge for yourselves. Not wait for a denominational statement. Not wait until a consensus forms among your peers. Not wait for someone with more technical credibility to tell you what moment this is. The waiting for permission is itself the failure being named.

#### Count the cost

I want to put one guardrail on all of this, because everything I have written so far pushes in one direction and the Scriptures do not leave it there.

Jesus also tells two short stories about estimation. A man begins a tower, runs out of money, and cannot finish it, and everyone who walks past mocks him. A king considers whether ten thousand can meet twenty thousand.

Notice what these are not. They are not arguments against building. Both assume the project. The failure is beginning without honest estimation, and the specific damage is public humiliation in front of everyone who watched you start.

I have been on the wrong side of that one. In 2025 I told a leadership team a build would take three months. I had spent two years training them to expect things much faster than that, and I was confident. Then I hit a part of the work I did not understand, and a week in I gave a demo that did not work. I diagnosed the problem quickly and I delivered the whole thing in about six weeks, which was well inside what I had promised. It did not matter. The room had decided in week one, and I never got it back.

The lesson was not that I moved too early. It was that I announced a tower I could not yet see the top of, in front of people who were watching. Shrewdness includes knowing the difference.

So: build. But size it to be finished, and say out loud what it will actually cost.

#### What I am not saying

Three things, plainly, because this kind of argument is easy to abuse and you should be able to see me refusing to.

I am not saying the moment Jesus tells you to read is this technology. It is not. In Luke 12 it is his own presence, and reading a market window into that is a category error. What transfers is a claim about how fear and competence and timing relate to each other in decisions people actually have to make. That structure is portable. The urgency of the kingdom is not on loan to anybody's product.

I am not saying that faithfulness requires adopting this. It requires deciding about it. Those are different, and a church that deliberates carefully and concludes it will use very little of this has done the thing I am asking for. What I am against is drift, in either direction.

And I have an obvious interest here. I build these systems for a living, and I sell exactly the kind of training I have just argued you need. You should weigh what I have written accordingly. I would rather say that plainly than have you notice it and wonder why I did not.

#### What shrewdness actually looks like this year

If I had to reduce all of it to a sequence, it would be this, and the order matters more than any single part.

**Decide what this is allowed to touch, before anyone uses it for anything that matters.** In your own words, in language your staff would actually use, written down. Not a policy nobody reads. A short set of lines about what does not go into these tools, particularly anything about the vulnerable people in your care, and what always requires a human being.

This is the innocence half, and there is real urgency in it. Every week you do not decide, your staff are deciding individually, in private, with the best intentions. Nobody is being unreasonable. They are trying to do their jobs and a tool showed up that helps. But information about real people is leaving your organization, nobody chose that, and there is no way to walk it back.

**Then make somewhere for people to actually learn.** A bounded place, real work, real stakes, where being wrong is survivable and someone is paying attention to what happens. This is the oil. Buy it before midnight.

**Then form people, at a scale that feels excessive.** Not a workshop. Enough hours that a practice forms. I have watched three years of astonishing demonstrations produce almost no change in behavior, in people I know well, who wanted it to work. Demonstration does not form anybody. It never has, in any part of your ministry.

**Then, and only then, build.** By that point you will know what to build, because the people who will use it will have been working with it for months and can tell you.

Everyone wants to start at the end. The tools are the interesting part and the part with a price attached. Starting there is the tower with no foundation.

#### The sentence I cannot get past

The children of this age are shrewder in dealing with their own generation than the children of light.

That is not a compliment to the children of this age. It is a rebuke to us, and it is observably accurate right now. The commercial world is deliberating about this with enormous seriousness. Meanwhile the institutions whose entire work runs on trust, which is the thing this technology most directly threatens, are largely doing neither. Not deciding what is permitted. Not building anything. Not shrewd, and not particularly innocent either. Just late.

We have a command with two halves. We have spent a long time treating one of them as optional and calling that faithfulness.

It is not the safe error. It is just the one that looks better while it costs you.

## 5. What shrewdness actually looks like

### What shrewdness actually looks like

*Companion to "Shrewd as serpents." For church leaders. ~3,100 words.*

---

In the first piece I argued that Scripture commands two things and we have been treating one of them as optional. Innocence without shrewdness is not faithfulness. It is negligence with better manners.

Fair enough as an argument. But an argument that ends there is worthless, because shrewdness is a word about means, and a claim about means that stays abstract is not a claim about anything.

So here is my attempt to say exactly what it consists of, for a church, this year. Not principles. The actual content of the competence.

I will start with what a shrewd leader knows, because most of the mistakes are knowledge failures rather than courage failures. Then what a shrewd church does. Then, at some length, what it refuses, because refusal is part of the competence and not a limit on it.

#### What a shrewd leader knows

##### That the tool is worst at the thing everyone asks about first

Every conversation I have with a pastor arrives at sermons within four minutes.

Here is the honest answer. These systems can produce a competent, structured, theologically unobjectionable sermon on almost any text in about ninety seconds. It will be usable. It will also be assembled from the average of everything ever written about that passage, which means it will contain no encounter, no risk, and nothing that came from you sitting with the text until it did something to you.

A shrewd leader understands why that matters, and it is not squeamishness. Preaching is not the transmission of content about a text. If it were, the machine would be fine at it. Preaching is a person who has been changed by a passage standing in front of people they know and telling them what they found. The congregation is not receiving information. They are watching someone who has been somewhere.

The average of the internet has not been anywhere.

So the sermon question is the wrong question, and the fact that it comes first is itself the tell. It reveals that the leader is thinking of this as a writing tool, which is the least interesting and most dangerous frame available.

##### That the actual leverage is memory, not efficiency

Here is what I think most churches have never noticed about themselves.

You are an institution whose entire theology is continuity. You baptize people and expect to bury them. You tell a story that spans generations. And you have catastrophic amnesia.

A pastor takes a call. Within eighteen months, most of what the previous pastor knew is simply gone. Not the records. The knowledge. Which family is still hurting from a split fifteen years ago. Why the trustees will not discuss the parking lot. Which of the older members quietly funds most of the benevolence. What was tried in 2011 and why it failed. Who has been asked to serve too many times and who has never been asked once.

A long-tenured member dies and takes forty years of congregational history with her, and nobody thought to ask.

You have twenty years of sermons in a folder, thirty years of newsletters in a box, session minutes going back further than that, mission trip reports, annual reports, prayer chains, funeral homilies. All of it is a record of what this congregation has believed and done and suffered, and none of it is reachable. Nobody can ask it anything.

The shrewd leader sees that as the asset. Not because it makes anything faster, but because the thing this technology is actually good at is making a large, scattered body of material answerable. That is not an efficiency play. It is an antidote to the specific way churches lose themselves.

##### That something is already answering questions about your church

Somebody in your community asked a machine about your church this month. Someone considering visiting, someone whose kid is thinking about your youth group, a reporter, a family looking for a funeral.

It answered. It did not say it was unsure. And it answered from whatever it could reach, which is your website, your Google listing, a couple of reviews, and anything anyone else has said about you. Not your actual theology. Not what you have learned in twenty years of being in that neighborhood. Not the sermon series that would have told them exactly who you are.

You do not get to opt out. The only decision available is whether the thing answering has anything real to work with.

##### That the cost curve moved and nobody told you

The kind of system that used to cost a church a quarter of a million dollars can now be built for a fraction of that. That is not marketing; it is the actual reason I have a company.

Practically, this means capabilities that were previously available only to churches with a communications department are now available to a congregation of ninety. That window will not stay open in this form. Right now the tools are cheap, the stakes are low, and being wrong is survivable. Both of those conditions are temporary in opposite directions.

##### That someone in your congregation is already fluent

There is a twenty-six-year-old in your pews using this six hours a day at work. There is a nurse in your congregation whose hospital has already run the whole governance conversation you are dreading. There is probably a schoolteacher living through the most intense version of this debate happening anywhere.

Churches consistently reach for an outside consultant while ignoring three people in the fourth row who know more than the consultant does about how this actually goes in an institution.

Shrewdness is noticing what you already have.

#### What a shrewd church does

##### Writes down its lines before anyone needs them

Not a policy document. Half a page, in your own words, that a volunteer could read and follow.

What never goes into these tools: anything a person told you in confidence, anything about a minor, anything from a pastoral conversation, anything from a counseling session, giving records tied to names, personnel matters.

What always has a human being on it: anything sent under the pastor's name, anything going to a family in grief, anything that will be read from the front, anything involving a child.

The reason to do this before rather than after is not legal. It is that your staff are currently deciding it individually, in private, at nine at night, on their own accounts, with the best intentions. They are pasting in the hardest things to write, which are exactly the things involving actual people, because those are the tasks where help is most wanted. Nobody is being unreasonable. But information about people in your care is leaving your church, nobody chose that, and it cannot be recalled.

##### Picks a real sandbox and staffs it

A bounded piece of real work, with real stakes, where being wrong is recoverable and someone is paying attention.

Concretely, the good candidates in a church are the ones with volume and low pastoral risk. The newsletter. The bulletin. Event descriptions. Grant applications. Meeting minutes. First drafts of routine correspondence. The website copy nobody has had time to rewrite since 2019. Making twenty years of sermons searchable.

The bad candidates are the ones people reach for first: anything pastoral, anything for a family in crisis, anything that involves speaking to a person about their own life.
