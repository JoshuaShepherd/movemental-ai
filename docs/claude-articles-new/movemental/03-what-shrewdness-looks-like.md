# What Shrewdness Actually Looks Like: A Field Guide for Churches

What makes it a sandbox rather than just usage is that somebody is watching and writing down what happens. Which tasks got better. Which got worse. Where somebody caught something wrong. Where somebody nearly did not.

##### Forms people at a scale that feels excessive

This is the part I would defend hardest, because I have three years of evidence and it all points the same way.

I have shown this technology to hundreds of people, most of them free, most of them people who trusted me and wanted it to work. They were astonished almost every time. Almost none of them adopted anything.

My best friend, for whom I spent five days learning his profession so the demonstration would sit inside his actual work, was impressed and did not touch AI again for two years.

**Demonstration does not form anybody.** You already know this. It is why you do not disciple people with a really good sermon. It is why your best volunteers were formed over years by somebody who stayed with them. Nothing about this technology suspends the way human beings actually learn.

So the honest number of hours is much larger than anyone wants it to be, it has to be with people rather than a video, and it has to be on their own real work rather than on examples. At Youthfront we are doing a hundred hours across every team in the organization. Not a pilot group. Not the technically inclined. Everyone.

A church can do a smaller version. But if the plan is a two-hour workshop, the plan is to have had a workshop.

##### Puts its own house in order first

Before any tool, ask who knows what around here, and what happens when they leave.

Most of the fragmentation in a church is not technological. It is that the person who knows why the Hendersons give is not the person writing the appeal. The person who was on the mission trip is not the person telling the story. The person who remembers the 2011 attempt is retired.

A tool does not fix that. People deciding to write things down fixes that, and then a tool makes it reachable. In the wrong order you get an expensive system that indexes nothing.

##### Talks to the people who already know

The nurse. The teacher. The twenty-six-year-old. Ask them what they have watched go well and badly, and what they wish their institution had decided earlier.

You will get better counsel in ninety minutes than most churches get from a consultant, and you will have done something more important than gathering information, which is to signal that this is a congregational conversation rather than an office decision.

##### Says out loud what it is doing

If your newsletter is drafted with AI assistance and edited by a person, say so once, plainly, somewhere findable.

There is a version of this that is fear management and a version that is shrewdness. The shrewd version understands that trust is the only asset a church has that cannot be rebuilt quickly, and that the way you lose it is not by using a tool. It is by having used it quietly and being found out. Disclosure is cheap now and priceless later.

#### What a shrewd church refuses

I want to be careful not to present refusal as the safety half of this. Knowing what a tool is for includes knowing what it is not for, and getting that wrong is a failure of competence, not just of nerve.

**Anything that is constituted by presence.** A hospital visit. Sitting with someone after a diagnosis. The conversation in the parking lot after the meeting. These are not tasks with outputs. The presence is the substance, and a more efficient version of it is not a version of it.

**Anything a person said to you in confidence.** No exceptions and no clever workarounds. Not summarized, not anonymized, not "just to help me think it through." The moment a congregant's disclosure becomes input, you have changed what confession means in your church, and you will not be able to change it back.

**Discernment.** A machine can lay out considerations, and that is genuinely useful. It cannot weigh them, because weighing requires knowing what this congregation has already been through and what it can bear. If a decision requires knowing your people, the tool is not a participant.

**Prayer.** I am not going to argue this one.

**Anything where the person on the other end thinks they are getting you.** A note to a grieving family. A response to someone in crisis. If they believe a person attended to them and no person did, you have not saved time. You have taken something from them without telling them.

**And anything involving a child, until you have thought about it much harder than you want to.** Photos, stories, safety records, anything a minor said. Churches have learned expensive lessons about child safety in the last thirty years and every one of those lessons applies here in a new form.

Notice that these refusals are not arbitrary and they are not about risk tolerance. They mark the places where the work is constituted by a human being doing it. That is a knowable line, and knowing where it falls is precisely the competence I am arguing for.

#### The first ninety days, concretely

If you did nothing else this year, this would be enough, and it is genuinely achievable for a church of any size.

**Weeks one and two.** Three or four leaders, separately, answer the same three questions on paper. What should this never touch here. Where would it obviously help. What are we afraid of. Then compare. The disagreements are the whole exercise, and there will be more of them than you expect.

**Weeks three to six.** Draft half a page of lines. Read it aloud to your staff and let them argue with it. Revise. The read-aloud is not a formality; anything that cannot be read aloud without qualification is not written clearly enough to be followed.

**Weeks seven to ten.** Pick two tasks with volume and low pastoral risk. Give them to two people. Ask them to keep a running note of what actually happened, including the near misses.

**Weeks eleven and twelve.** Sit with the notes. Decide what graduates into normal practice, what stops, and what still needs watching. Then talk to your congregation about what you have decided and why, before anyone asks.

Ninety days, no budget, no vendor. And at the end you will have something almost no church has, which is a decision.

#### The part I have to disclose

I build these systems and I sell exactly the kind of training I have just described. You should weigh what I have written accordingly, and I would rather say it than have you find it.

I will add one thing that cuts against my own interest. Most of what I have listed above does not require me or anyone like me. The half page, the sandbox, the conversation with the nurse and the teacher, the ninety days — a church can do all of it alone, this quarter, for nothing. If you do only that, you will be ahead of nearly every congregation I have talked to in three years.

The place outside help earns its keep is later, when you want to make a large body of material actually reachable, which is a real technical problem that does not solve itself. Not at the start.

#### Why this is shrewdness and not caution

Read back over the list and notice what is not in it. There is nothing about protecting yourself, nothing about liability, nothing about waiting to see how it shakes out.

Every item is about competence. Knowing what the tool is good at. Knowing what you already have. Knowing who in the room already knows. Knowing where the line falls and why it falls there. Building the capacity to judge before the moment when judgment is needed, because that capacity cannot be borrowed at midnight.

That is what practical wisdom has always consisted of, in Proverbs and in Luke and in every ordinary decision your elders have ever made well. It is the intelligence of a person who has to act, in a situation that will not wait, with responsibility for people who did not choose the situation either.

The children of this age are shrewder in dealing with their own generation. They are, at the moment, and it is not because they are smarter than you. It is because they decided it was their business to be competent about this, and we decided it was somebody else's business.

It is not somebody else's business. There is a twenty-six-year-old in your pews who has already formed her habits, and nobody in your church has said one word to her about it.

### How It Actually Works

*A companion to the Ferguson memo. Walks through the mechanism — the four moves that take Movemental from one platform to a hundred-node network the internet can see. Written for senior leaders, not for technologists. No SEO acronyms. No GEO jargon. Just what happens, in what order, and what it produces.*

---

It works in four moves. The first one is the hardest. The other three are the same move, repeated, with multiplication and linking added at each step.

#### Move One — Build one platform that does the whole job

Start with one author. In our case, Alan Hirsch. Build him a digital platform that does *everything* the public-credibility and authorship part of his calling actually requires. Not a website. Not a marketing surface. The full apparatus:

- His entire body of work, made navigable — the books, the talks, the interviews, the frameworks, the long-form articles — organized around the pillars of his thinking rather than around the chronology of publication.
- A content engine that lets new pieces be produced at the depth he would have produced them himself, in his actual voice, anchored in his actual corpus, never inventing positions he has not taken.
- Pathways — guided journeys through his thinking for a reader who wants to learn missional church, or apostolic movement, or APEST, in the order Alan himself would teach it.
- Courses that run as cohorts, not as content libraries, because formation requires community, not consumption.
- A newsletter line that maintains his ongoing presence in subscribers' reading life.
- An AI conversation agent that can sit inside a pathway or course and hold the formation questions Alan himself would ask — bounded to his corpus, faithful to his voice, refusing to invent.

The technical phrase for what makes a platform like this credible to the systems that mediate the internet is *Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trust.* You do not need to know the acronym. What it means in plain language is this: **does this platform demonstrate that the author has actually done the work, knows it deeply, is recognized by peers, and can be trusted to tell the truth about it?**

For Alan, the answer is already yes — in the embodied world. He has spent thirty-plus years planting, training, writing, teaching. He has published thirteen books that other practitioners cite and build on. Peers like Brad, Dave, Mike Breen, Neil Cole vouch for him in the field constantly. The credibility *exists.* It just has not been made structurally visible online in a form the modern internet can recognize.

What the platform does is make that already-real credibility *legible.* When a reader lands on any Alan article, they can see the breadth of his work, the depth of his frameworks, the named peers who teach alongside him, the cohorts he has formed, the corpus he is building on. The platform is not creating credibility. It is making credibility readable to systems that, before this moment, could only see whether a page existed, not whether the person behind it had done the work.

That is move one. Build one platform that does the whole job for one credible voice.

**What changes when you do it.** A single faithful practitioner — someone who has spent thirty years staying local, embodied, sent — finally has a home online that matches the actual weight of what they have done. They become findable. They become readable. They become someone a curious twenty-eight-year-old church planter in Birmingham or Bangalore can actually encounter, learn from, and apprentice into.

This is necessary. But by itself, it is not yet a movement.

#### Move Two — Multiply to a second platform, and link the two

Build the second platform for Brad Brisco. Same architecture. Same depth. Same disciplines: corpus-grounded, voice-faithful, formation-anchored, cohort-driven. The build takes a fraction of the time the first one did, because the underlying patterns have been figured out and the AI scaffolding that drafts the structural pieces — pathway outlines, course shells, corpus indexing, voice configuration — has been built.

But this is where something becomes possible that was not possible at move one. **You can link them.**

Here is what linking actually means, in plain terms. When an Alan article cites Brad's work on bivocational ministry, the citation is not just a hyperlink. It is a *structural* declaration: <em>this author corroborates this other author on this specific topic.</em> When Brad's article on bivocational practice cites Alan's framework on APEST, the same declaration runs in reverse. When a course on Alan's platform references Brad's field practitioners as a learning destination, that is a third declaration. When both platforms share the same canonical definitions of the same terms — *missional, apostolic, movemental, sent, embodied* — that is a fourth.

What you have produced is not two websites. You have produced **two credibility nodes that publicly reinforce each other.**

This matters for one specific reason. The systems that now mediate the internet — Google's retrieval ranking, AI assistants, the conversational interfaces that the next generation increasingly uses to find anything — do not evaluate any single page in isolation. They evaluate networks. **Authority transfers through links between credible sources.** When a person already trusted by these systems publicly corroborates another person, the trust extends to the second person, and the corroboration extends to whatever they both stand for.

This is not a technical trick. It is the digital expression of something that has always been true offline: *we trust people partly because of who vouches for them.* In the embodied world, when Alan introduces Brad to a denomination's leadership team and says "Brad has been doing this work as long as I have, and we have stood next to each other in it for twenty years," the introduction transfers credibility. The internet, until recently, had no way to see this. Now it does, but only if the relationship is made structurally visible — and that requires both platforms to exist, both to publish substantively, and both to link to each other deliberately and reciprocally.

**What changes when you do it.** You stop having two single platforms competing for attention in the same field. You have a small federation that, from the perspective of the systems mediating the internet, looks like *two corroborating authorities on the same body of work.* That federation is qualitatively different from the sum of its parts. It is the first appearance of something that did not exist before: a structurally visible movement.

#### Move Three — Multiply to a hundred, with shared infrastructure

Now repeat the move. Build platforms for a hundred senior voices — Dave Ferguson, Megan Good, Liz Ríos, Lance Ford, JR Woodward, Daniel Yang, Sandra Maria Van Opstal, Mike Breen, Hugh Halter, Caesar Kalinowski, on and on. People whose credibility has been earned by thirty years of staying local, embodied, sent, faithful. People who never became influencers because they were too busy doing the actual work.

The reason this scale is possible — and was not possible four years ago — is what AI has made economically feasible. The architecture each platform requires used to cost $250,000 to $750,000 per organization to build and a multi-million-dollar coordination budget to link. With AI-assisted engineering, the same architecture is buildable for roughly $50,000 per platform and the coordination cost compresses with it. We are not pretending this is free. We are saying it has dropped two orders of magnitude in the window we are currently building inside.

But the move that matters at scale is not just the multiplication. It is the *shared infrastructure.* All hundred platforms operate on:

- A shared vocabulary. When any platform uses a term like *missional, apostolic, sent community,* the term carries the same canonical definition across the entire network. The reader on platform forty-seven sees the same definition the reader on platform three sees, with the same attribution to wherever in the network the definition originates.
- A shared schema. Each article, each course, each pathway, each author, each book is structured in the same machine-readable way. This is not visible to readers but it is what allows the systems that mediate the internet to see the entire network as one coherent body of work rather than a hundred unrelated websites.
- Shared citation conventions. When a platform references another author's framework, the reference cites the canonical source. When a platform extends another author's idea, the extension is acknowledged. The honest formulation — *Alan does not say this directly, but his framework suggests it* — is the rule, not the exception.
- Shared formation disciplines. Every artifact across every platform passes through the same four-question test: where is the dissonance, what is the action, what is the reflection structure, where is community gestured at. No artifact gets published that has collapsed into information-only mode. The network refuses to produce content that does not aim at formation.
- Shared refusals. No AI authorship under any human's name. No invented quotes. No fabricated positions. No voice synthesis without explicit consent. The same boundaries everywhere.

These shared elements are not enforced through bureaucracy. They are encoded into the platform infrastructure itself — into the templates that draft articles, the prompts that propose pathways, the editorial gates that pieces pass through before publication. **The discipline is structural, not procedural.** A platform on the Movemental network cannot easily produce a low-credibility artifact, because the system is designed to refuse one.

**What changes when you do it.** You now have a hundred credibility nodes operating on shared infrastructure with reciprocal linking. From the perspective of the systems that mediate the internet, what was previously a hundred unrelated voices has become *one coherent field of practice with a hundred named contributors.* That field has internal corroboration density a single website cannot achieve and a small federation cannot achieve. It crosses a threshold.

#### Move Four — Link them as a network the internet can actually see

This is the move that completes the architecture. At a hundred nodes, with shared infrastructure and reciprocal linking, what you have built is no longer a federation. It is a *network with semantic density* — a phrase that means the network speaks one language about its subject, cross-references itself extensively, and is recognized by the systems that mediate the internet as the authoritative source on that subject.

The practical consequence is this. When a thirty-year-old pastor in Birmingham asks an AI assistant *how do I plant a reproducing church,* the systems that answer that question draw on what they recognize as authoritative. Today, what they recognize as authoritative on that question is whatever happens to have been published prominently in the formats they were trained on — much of which is shallow, much of which is wrong, much of which is the work of people who have never planted a church. After this network exists for two to three years and accumulates the corroboration density that comes from a hundred credible voices teaching the same body of work in coordinated form, *the answer the AI returns becomes the network's answer.* Not because we manipulated retrieval. Because we made structurally visible the work the network has actually done.

The same is true when the question is asked in Spanish. Or Portuguese. Or Mandarin. Or Korean. Or German. Or French. The translation move is part of move four — once the English-language network has reached the threshold, the translated platforms inherit the credibility structure and seed the same network in other language ecosystems. A Spanish-speaking pastor asking *cómo multiplicar una comunidad de discípulos* finds the same depth of thinking in the same coordinated form. Not because we paid for visibility. Because the deepest thinking on apostolic multiplication, made structurally visible in the language of the asker, is what the systems retrieve.

This is also the move at which the network becomes something more than the sum of its nodes. The shared vocabulary deepens. New collaborations form across nodes that did not exist before — a theologian on platform twelve writes with a practitioner on platform sixty-three on a topic neither would have addressed alone, and the joint piece appears on both platforms and reinforces both nodes. Cohorts form across platforms. Apprenticeships extend across the network. The hundred voices begin to behave, online, the way they have always behaved offline — as a *real movement,* in active relationship, mutually reinforcing.

**What changes when you do it.** The apostolic conversation gets carried into the next twenty years in the voices that have actually done the work. The next generation, asking the questions the previous generation asked of mentors, finds the right answers. Faithful obscurity becomes visible without becoming influence. The architecture apostolic mission has needed for a generation finally exists.

#### The road map, condensed

| Move | What you do | What it produces |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Build one platform that does the whole job for one voice | A faithful practitioner finally findable online at the weight of their actual work |
| 2 | Build a second, link them reciprocally | Two corroborating authorities — the first appearance of structural visibility for a movement |
| 3 | Build to a hundred with shared infrastructure | One coherent field of practice with a hundred named contributors and the corroboration density a single site cannot reach |
| 4 | Operate as a linked network across languages | The network becomes what the systems mediating the internet recognize as the authoritative source on the body of work |

#### What this is and is not

This is not a marketing strategy. It is the digital expression of the same principle that has organized every apostolic movement in history: *small enough to be intimate, large enough to be a system; framework without control; multiplication through reciprocal vouching; credibility carried by relationships among people who have done the work.* What is new is not the principle. What is new is that the principle now has a digital expression that the systems mediating the internet can recognize.

This is also not a technology play. The technology is the substrate, not the story. AI is what makes the build economically possible at this scale; it is not what produces the credibility. The credibility is produced by the thirty years of work the hundred voices have already done. The platform makes that work readable to a generation that does its discovery through systems that, before this architecture existed, could not see it.

And this is not a single-organization product. It is the infrastructure layer for a movement that has already existed for a generation in embodied form. We are building the digital architecture the apostolic church has not yet had — and that, on the other side of this moment, the apostolic church will not be able to function without.

The how is the four moves. The why is everything the Ferguson memo says. The when is the next thirty-six months, because that is the window in which the substrate of authority is changing and the relational substrate Movemental requires can still be assembled at a pace that becomes impossible once the category becomes visible to competitors.

That is how it works.

##### A human network for the age of AI

*A talk. About fifteen minutes, read at a measured pace.*

#### Start with what is already happening

I want to begin with something that is already happening inside your organization, whether or not anyone decided it should.
