# Movemental: A Human Network for the Age of AI

**Solutions** comes last because Solutions without Skills is replacement. You've put AI into the hands of people who can't steward it, inside infrastructure you didn't rebuild for the new conditions. That is the MIT report's 95% failure mode, but for organizations whose failures cost them their reason to exist.

AI doesn't only demand new policies. It demands a rebuild of how mission-driven organizations actually work. We sequence that rebuild so it doesn't destroy what it claims to improve. The conveyor belt is the thesis. We don't push anyone along it; the logic does the moving. The work is ordered because the consequences of skipping a stage compound, and for the organizations we serve, the compound interest is paid in credibility.

#### Notes on what this version does and doesn't yet do

- It leads with the diagnostic ("AI is already in the room") rather than the architecture. The architecture is the *answer*, not the opening.
- It puts the credibility-is-the-product distinction at position two, because that's the asymmetry that justifies the entire investment of attention.
- It states each stage *as the consequence of the previous stage*, not as an item on a list. The conveyor belt is the thesis; the four stages are how the conveyor belt expresses itself.
- It cites the MIT report once, briefly, for the failure-rate anchor. If used in writing, the citation should be tightened with the exact figure and publication date.
- It deliberately does not name pricing for Sandbox, Skills, or Solutions. The narrative's job is logic, not commerce; pricing belongs in the pricing page, not in the three-minute version of the thesis.
- It deliberately does not name the Movement Voices, the scenius, or the ten-year picture. Those belong to a different narrative — the *why we exist* narrative, not the *why these four stages in this order* narrative.

#### What could change in v0.2

- The opening line. "AI is already inside your organization" is functional but generic. A more specific opening — naming a particular use case the listener will recognize from their own staff — would land harder.
- The three illustrations in paragraph three (church/homily, nonprofit/donor letters, seminary/feedback). These can be sharpened with actual incidents that have already happened publicly, if cited carefully.
- The Solutions paragraph. The MIT reference is doing a lot of work; either the citation needs to be airtight, or the paragraph needs a second supporting beat.
- The closing image — "the compound interest is paid in credibility." This is the line the listener should leave with. Test it aloud and see whether it lands or whether it lands too clever.

## 9. Problem stories

### Problem stories

*Each one: who it happens to, what happens, what it costs. Then what it takes without AI, and what it takes with AI. Generalized — not case studies.*

---

#### 1. Finding what you already wrote

An author who has written fifteen books wants the passage where he made an argument best. He knows he wrote it, roughly when, probably in a chapter. He searches for forty minutes across publisher PDFs and two websites that no longer exist, does not find it, and writes a new version from memory. The new version is weaker than the original and he knows it while he is writing it. What he wants is to be able to ask his own body of work a question.

**Without AI.** Someone reads the entire corpus and indexes it by idea rather than by keyword — months of skilled work, repeated whenever the corpus grows. No author pays for that. So the searching does not happen, and over time a body of work narrows to the parts its author can still recall.

**With AI.** A few weeks to convert and load the books properly, with a person handling every file that will not convert cleanly. After that it is a search: he asks, the passage comes back with its page, and he decides whether he still holds it.

---

#### 2. Spoken work that cannot be searched

A speaker has given roughly four hundred talks over thirty years. Conference sessions, interviews, long Q&As. Most of it was never written down, and much of it is clearer than anything he has published. The recordings exist on a hard drive. Nobody can search audio for a half-remembered sentence, including him.

**Without AI.** Professional transcription costs a few hundred dollars per talk. Four hundred talks is a budget line no author or publisher has ever approved, so it does not get done at any point. The material stays technically preserved and practically unreachable.

**With AI.** Transcription with speaker labels and timestamps, unclear passages marked rather than guessed at, and a person who knows the field correcting names and terms. Thirty years of spoken work becomes readable, quotable, and teachable.

---

#### 3. Not knowing whether it is still him

A reader has followed a writer for twenty years. She opens his newest article and cannot tell whether he wrote it or a model did. The prose is good, which does not help. She reads more carefully than she used to, enjoys it less, and eventually stops subscribing without telling anyone why.

**Without AI.** She never had to ask the question. That is the difference this technology made, and it applies to every writer with a public byline whether or not they use the tools.

**With AI.** State plainly and publicly how the work is made — he writes every word, AI gathers and structures, sources are named, nothing is invented — and then keep the practice matching the statement. It costs nothing while it is true and cannot be recovered once it is found to be false.

---

#### 4. Work that exists in one language

A pastor in São Paulo leads a network of forty churches and has read a summary of a book, not the book. The book is in English and will stay in English. Publishers translate titles expected to earn back a translation advance; most authors never clear that threshold, so most of their work is never translated at all.

**Without AI.** Nothing happens. Not slowly, not expensively — for authors below the commercial threshold there is no route to translation at any price. Their readers outside English argue with summaries.

**With AI.** A translation drafted from an approved piece, then read line by line by a person who speaks the language, with that person named. The checking step is not optional: the text goes out under the author's name in a language he cannot read.

---

#### 5. Publishing into a void

A writer publishes an article that took him three weeks. It has no headings, no summary, no links to the other forty pieces that would make sense of it, and no structure a search or answer engine can read. It reaches the people already on his mailing list and nobody else. He has been publishing this way for twenty years.

**Without AI.** He hires someone who understands both his work and how discovery functions, and pays them to work back through two decades of material. Few authors do this. The result is a ceiling: the work reaches people who were already convinced, and the people it was written for never encounter it.

**With AI.** Headings, summaries, structured metadata, and internal links applied to finished pieces — after the writing, never before it. Starting from search demand instead produces different writing within a year.

---

#### 6. The talk that never became an article

A teacher gives the clearest version of his central idea to a room of two hundred people on a Tuesday. He intends to write it up. Writing it up is a week he does not have, so the recording sits in a folder. Over a career this happens dozens of times.

**Without AI.** He hires a ghostwriter, which creates the authorship problem in exchange for solving the time problem, or he finds the week, which he does not have. Usually neither happens.

**With AI.** A transcript, then a proposed structure built only from what he actually said, then he rewrites it himself. The rewriting is where he thinks it through again, and occasionally decides he no longer agrees with his own talk.

---

#### 7. Being represented by a model

A seminary student asks a general-purpose model what a well-known author thinks about a subject. The model answers confidently, in something close to his voice, with a position he has never held. She cites it in a paper. The author never learns this happened.

**Without AI.** It did not happen. There is now no way to audit it, correct it, or opt out of it.

**With AI.** Put a grounded answer where the question gets asked — built only from what he actually wrote, with book and chapter attached, saying plainly when the corpus does not cover the question, and never producing a quotation he did not write. It does not stop misrepresentation elsewhere. It makes the accurate version the easiest one to find.

---

#### 8. Staff already using it, with no policy

A nonprofit executive director has eleven staff. At least six use AI daily. One drafts donor letters with it, one summarizes meetings that include pastoral detail, and one is pasting material into a free tool with unclear data handling. There is no policy. Nobody has raised it at a staff meeting. She learned about most of this by accident.

**Without AI.** She is already in the with-AI situation. The only variable is whether anyone is looking. With no policy, practice runs ahead of conviction by a year or more, and the first review happens after an incident.
