Youthfront is a nonprofit in Kansas City. [ONE SENTENCE: founding year, what they do, roughly how many staff and how many young people a year.] I have known people there for years, which is the only reason this engagement happened the way it did.

They did not come to us with an AI problem. They came with grant money and a sense that they should be doing something, and no clear picture of what.

That is the most common starting condition I encounter, and it is a better one than it sounds.

#### The situation before

Here is what was actually true when we started, and I suspect it is true where you are.

AI was already inside the organization. Nobody had approved it, nobody had forbidden it, and nobody had discussed it. Staff were using personal accounts for work tasks, because the tools help and because no one had said otherwise. [SPECIFIC: the real version, without identifying anyone.]

The tasks they reached for were the hard ones. Not the trivial ones. The hardest thing on a nonprofit staff member's desk is usually a piece of writing about a person: a donor situation that needs delicacy, a difficult family circumstance, a story from a program, a personnel matter. Those are exactly the tasks where a tool that drafts well feels like relief.

Which means the information most likely to be pasted into an unapproved account is the information about real people, and at Youthfront a significant share of those people are minors.

Nothing had gone wrong. I want to be clear about that, because it is the part that makes this hard to act on. There had been no incident, no breach, no complaint. And the absence of a bad day was being read, understandably, as evidence that there was nothing yet to decide.

Second thing that was true, and it had nothing to do with AI. Their institutional memory was scattered in the ordinary way. The person who knows why a donor gives is not the person writing the appeal. The person who was at camp is not the person telling the story. Twenty-some years of program knowledge lived in people's heads and in folders nobody outside a given team could find. [SPECIFIC EXAMPLE, non-identifying.]

That second problem is the one that determines whether any tool is worth buying. A capable system pointed at a fragmented organization indexes the fragmentation.

#### What we decided to do, and why in that order

We did not start with tools. We started with a decision, then a place to practice, then training, and the tools come last. That sequence is the whole method and the order is not a preference.

**Safety first.** Not a policy document. A set of lines the organization actually holds, in its own language, that a part-time summer staffer could read and follow.

The reason to do this before anything else is not legal exposure. It is that in the absence of a decision, every staff member is making the decision privately, at nine at night, with the best intentions. Nobody is being unreasonable. But information about young people is leaving the organization, no one chose that, and there is no way to recall it.

We produced [WHAT THE GUIDEBOOK ACTUALLY CONTAINS: the layers, roughly how long it is, who wrote which parts]. The part I care most about is the section on minors, which sets a higher bar rather than a comparable one, and the exception path, which matters because a rule with no exception path gets ignored rather than followed.

**Then a sandbox.** A bounded place to use this on real work, with real stakes, where being wrong is survivable and somebody is watching what happens.

[WHAT WENT IN THE SANDBOX: which teams, which tasks, over what period.] The candidates were chosen for volume and low pastoral risk. The things deliberately kept out were anything involving a young person's story, anything from a family conversation, and anything that would go out under someone's name to a person in distress.

**Then training, at a scale that surprises people.** A hundred hours, in person, across ten teams and every part of the organization. Not a pilot group, not the technically inclined staff, not the leadership. Everyone.

**Then tech.** We are not there yet, and that is on purpose. [CURRENT STATUS: what is being built, what is still in discussion.]

#### Why a hundred hours

This is the number people ask about, so here is the reasoning rather than a justification.

I spent three years demonstrating this technology to people who trusted me and wanted it to work. They were astonished nearly every time and almost none of them adopted anything. My best friend, for whom I spent five days learning his profession so the demonstration would sit inside his actual work, was impressed and did not touch AI again for two years.

Demonstration does not form anybody. Youthfront already knew this about every other kind of formation they do. Nobody at that organization believes a young person is discipled by one excellent talk.

So the training is not a workshop with follow-up. It is hours, in person, on people's own real work, in groups small enough that somebody notices when a person is quietly lost. [WHAT A SESSION ACTUALLY LOOKS LIKE: who is in the room, what happens, what is different between hour one and hour forty.]

A hundred hours is a lot to ask of a nonprofit. It is the most expensive part of what we do and I have not found a way around it that I believe in.

#### What this engagement did not do

Worth being precise, because a case study that implies more than happened is worse than none.

It did not make the organization more efficient in any way I would put a number on. It might, later. That was not the goal of this stage.

It did not build a custom system. Nothing has been engineered for Youthfront yet.

It did not solve the institutional memory problem. It surfaced it, and the organization now sees it, which is a prerequisite rather than a solution.

And it did not eliminate risk. There is no arrangement that does. What changed is that the decisions are now made in the open, by the organization, with a record, rather than individually and privately.

#### Whether your situation is the same shape

The test is not whether you are a nonprofit or whether you work with young people. It is these three:

Is AI already being used in your organization without anyone having decided anything about it. Almost certainly yes.

Do the hardest writing tasks on your staff's desks involve real people. If yes, that is what is being pasted into unapproved accounts.

Is your institutional knowledge in people's heads rather than anywhere reachable. If yes, no tool will fix it and buying one first will waste the money.

If all three are true, the order we used will probably work for you, and most of the first stage you can do yourselves this quarter without hiring anyone. Half a page of lines, read aloud to your staff, argued with, revised. A bounded place to practice. Someone paying attention. That is not a product. That is a decision, and it is the part that cannot be bought.
