# The Thing That Is Broken Is Wholeness: Fragmentation as the Root Problem

`fragmentationthesis.pdf` · 3,060 words

THE ROOT PROBLEM

The thing that is broken is wholeness. On fragmentation as the root problem Movemental addresses — what it means for a writer like Alan Hirsch and a church like Tampa Underground, how AI amplifies it, and the line between healing it and rebuilding Babel.

AUTHOR

Joshua Shepherd Founder, Movemental

Working theory Held for discussion

---

THE RO O T PRO BLEM

Underneath every problem we address, there is one problem: fragmentation. Whether the subject is an individual leader, a movement leader, or an organization like a church, a nonprofit, or a seminary, the thing that is actually broken is the same thing. The intelligence that constitutes that person or that body has been scattered. It is no longer whole. By intelligence we do not mean cleverness. We mean the whole of what a person or an organization knows and is — the accumulated understanding, frameworks, language, convictions, and hard-won wisdom that make them who they are, together with the web of relationships through which that understanding actually lives and moves in the world. This intelligence has two faces, and they are not separable. There is an informational face — what is known, what has been said, the body of work. And there is a relational face — who is known, how understanding moves between people, the web of trust through which knowledge is transmitted and received. A person or an organization in good health holds both as one. The knowing and the people the knowing is for are not in different places. They are integrated. Fragmentation is what happens when that integration breaks — when the intelligence that was once whole gets scattered across containers that no longer reference one another. The knowledge ends up in one set of places. The relationships end up in another. And neither can see the other. This paper makes that claim concrete, traces what AI does to it, and then names the danger hiding inside the cure.

1

---

For a writer like Alan Hirsch, the intelligence exists. It is just nowhere whole. Consider the actual state of one senior author's body of work. Alan Hirsch has spent more than thirty years writing, planting, training, and teaching. The understanding is real and it is vast. But ask a simple question — where is it? — and the answer is a list of disconnected containers: Thirteen-plus books, each owned by a different publisher under a different contract, their contents locked inside PDFs and print and incompatible ebook formats that do not speak to one another. Articles scattered across Missio Alliance, Forge sites, his own older websites, and platforms that may no longer exist. Talks that survive only as unindexed conference videos, loose audio files, or not at all. Podcast appearances dispersed across dozens of other people's feeds. Frameworks he originated — APEST, mDNA, the six elements — that now live more in other people's paraphrases than in any canonical home of his own. Interviews, forewords, course material, unpublished talks. And beneath all of it, a personal layer of notes, drafts, and half-finished thoughts in something like Evernote or a drawer. The fragmentation claim, made concrete, is this: there is no single place where Alan Hirsch's intelligence is whole. Not for a reader. Not for a researcher. Not for the next generation who will look for him. And — newly urgent — not for an AI system trying to represent him. The intelligence exists. It is shattered across forty containers, half of them decaying, none of them referencing the others, most of them invisible to the systems that now mediate how anything is found.

The work was whole in the person. It has been scattered into the world. What was once an integrated body of understanding is now an archaeology — and most people will never do the dig.

2

---

The same scattering has a second face: he cannot see the people his work is for. The relational fragment is harder to see than the informational one, but for an individual author it is just as real. Alan has an audience — people who have been formed by his work, who quote him, teach him, build on him, who would drop everything to learn from him. But that audience exists only as scattered traces: an email list gone stale, LinkedIn connections, conference attendees whose names were never captured, followers on platforms he does not control, readers who bought books through channels that never told him who they were, people who found him through someone else's podcast and whom he will never know exist. And here is the connection that matters. Because his work is scattered, he cannot see how anyone moves through it. He does not know that a particular reader found APEST through a blog post, then bought The Forgotten Ways, then took a course, then began teaching it in their own church. That journey — which is the entire point, the actual formation arc — is invisible to him, because the work the journey moves through is itself shattered across containers that do not talk to one another. So the relational fragment is not a second, separate problem. It is the shadow the informational fragment casts. You cannot see the people moving through the work when the work is not anywhere whole enough to be moved through.

A church like Tampa Underground has the same wound, in a body rather than a person. If the pattern is real, it should hold for an organization as cleanly as it holds for an individual. It does. The difference is only that an organization's intelligence is distributed across many heads and folders rather than one.

Their teaching lives in sermon videos no one can search. Their microchurch guides sit in Google Drive folders. Their theology lives in the founders' heads. Their actual practiced ecclesiology — the thing they have learned about doing church this way — is largely undocumented, because it was transmitted by apprenticeship rather than by writing. The intelligence of the Underground is real and it is considerable. It is also scattered across hundreds of heads and folders, and it is one bus-accident away from being lost in any given node.

Who is actually in the Underground? The microchurch leaders know their people. The central team knows the leaders. But there is no place where the whole body of relationships is visible at once. When a microchurch dies, its relational knowledge dies with it. When a leader leaves, the people they discipled become invisible to the network. The Underground's deepest asset — a living web of relationships through which formation actually happens — exists, but the body cannot see its own body. The pattern is identical to Alan's. An intelligence that was once whole — informationally and relationally — has been scattered into containers that do not reference one another, and the scattering of the knowledge and the scattering of the relationships are, again, the same event. What is true of one writer is true of one church. It is true of Forge America, whose decade of training wisdom lives in the slides of trainers who have cycled through, and whose every trained practitioner is a node of the movement that Forge can no longer see. It is true of a seminary, whose faculty intelligence retires out the door and whose alumni — its single richest relational asset — are reduced to entries in a development office's giving database.

4

---

In every case, an intelligence that was once whole has been scattered into containers that do not reference each other — and in every case, the scattering of what is known and the scattering of who is known are the same scattering. This is not a technology problem that happens to recur. It is one wound, appearing wherever human intelligence has been committed to systems that do not hold it together.

5

---

AI does not heal fragmentation by default. It feeds on it — and amplifies it. Here the paper turns from diagnosis to mechanism, because this is the part that makes the moment urgent rather than merely unfortunate. AI is a mirror. It reflects and amplifies whatever it is given. And what it is given, in the present condition, is fragmentation. When a fragmented self is presented to the mirror, the reflection that comes back is fragmented. When an author's work is scattered, an AI asked to represent that author assembles its answer from whatever shards happen to be visible — a paraphrase here, a half-remembered framework there, a secondhand summary somewhere else — and returns something that wears the author's name without carrying the author's coherence. The mirror cannot reflect a wholeness that is not present to it. It can only reflect, and amplify, the scattering. This is the mechanism beneath what everyone has learned to call slop. Slop is not simply lowquality output. Slop is fragmentation reflected and amplified. It is what the mirror necessarily produces when the self in front of it is in pieces. And because that amplified output then enters the world as new content — indexed, retrieved, trained on, cited — it becomes a further source of fragmentation for the next reflection. The mirror feeds on the scattering and excretes more of it.

A fragmented self, presented to a mirror, produces a fragmented reflection. Amplified fragmentation is slop. And slop is fragmentation, compounded.

This reframes what is at stake in the present moment for everyone Movemental serves. The danger is not that AI is too powerful. The danger is that AI faithfully mirrors and multiplies the condition it finds — and the condition it finds, in nearly every person and organization, is fragmentation. Left alone, the mirror does not assemble the scattered self. It scatters it further, faster, and at scale, while wearing the face of coherence.

6

---

Transformation happens only where redeemed information and redeemed relationship meet again. If fragmentation is the wound, the temptation is to think the cure is simply de-fragmentation — gather the scattered information into one place and call it healed. But a corpus made whole and left disconnected from its people is only a library. And a people gathered and left disconnected from the corpus is only a fan club. Neither of those transforms anyone. The actual mechanism of repair is more specific, and it is the load-bearing claim of this entire paper:

THE K EY STONE

Transformation becomes possible only at the point where redeemed informational intelligence and redeemed relational intelligence intersect again — and begin to produce new insight and meaningful interaction with one another.

This is not poetry; it is mechanics. Formation — the thing Alan actually cares about, the thing a church is for, the thing the whole enterprise exists to serve — happens only when a person moves through a coherent body of work in the company of other people moving through it, and that movement is visible enough to be shepherded. Information without relationship is inert. Relationship without information is empty. The intersection of the two — the corpus made whole, the people made visible, and the living traffic between them — is where formation lives, and it is the only place it lives. This is why making Alan's work navigable is necessary but not sufficient. This is why a CRM full of contacts is not a relationship. The work and the people have to be made whole and brought back into contact with each other, such that the author can see how a reader moves through the work, the reader can find the others on the same path, and new understanding is generated in the meeting. Re-integration, not mere consolidation, is the goal. The wound was the severing of the two. The healing is their reunion.

THE DAN GER IN SIDE THE CURE

Not all coherence heals. The danger is rebuilding Babel with better materials. Here the paper turns on itself, because the cure has a shadow and integrity requires naming it before anyone else does. Movemental is in the business of un-scattering. We reverse fragmentation. We make intelligence whole again and project it, coherently, into the world and into the systems that now mediate it. And there is an old story that should make us careful about exactly that. The tower of Babel is not, at root, a story about language. It is a story about a people who said let us make a name for ourselves and built a coherent tower toward heaven on their own terms. God scattered them — and the scattering is usually read as judgment, which it was. But notice what the judgment prevented. God did not scatter them because coherence is evil. God scattered them because their coherence was aimed at self-deification. The fragmentation was, in part, a mercy against a unified human will bent on replacing God. So the knife turns toward our own work. If Movemental makes a movement's intelligence whole — informationally and relationally coherent, amplified, projected as a unified signal into the substrate that now answers the world's questions — and that coherence is aimed, even slightly, at making a name for ourselves, then we have not reversed Babel. We have rebuilt the tower with better materials. That is the precise danger. The one thing more dangerous than reverberating incoherence is coherent godlessness. This is the real reason the refusals matter — and they are not ethics bolted onto the side of a product. The commitment never to author under a human's name without disclosure; the insistence that the platform serve embodied local mission rather than replace it; the refusal to let practitioners become mere content creators; the commitment that the network reflect the diversity of the Kingdom; the conviction that we are not the platform, the voices are — these are the discipline that keeps the un-scattering from becoming a tower. They are what keep coherence pointed away from itself.

8

---

There are two un-scatterings in Scripture. We want the second one. Babel is one reversal of fragmentation — and it is the wrong one. But there is a second, and it is the one this work is actually reaching for. At Pentecost the Spirit reverses Babel — but notice how. Not by making everyone speak one language. By making everyone understood in their own language. The coherence of Pentecost is not the coherence of uniformity. It is the coherence of mutual intelligibility across difference, gathered around the proclamation of God rather than the name of man. Babel flattens difference and points at itself. Pentecost honors difference and points away from itself. Both are un-scatterings. Only one is healing.

Babel-coherence is unified, self-naming, God-replacing. Pentecost-coherence is mutually intelligible, differencehonoring, God-proclaiming. The whole question is which one we are building.

This is why the multilingual commitment is not a feature and the diversity commitment is not a marketing line. They are the Pentecost pattern, structurally enacted. Translating the work so that a pastor in Bogotá finds it in her own tongue — making the deepest thinking on a subject understood in the language of the asker, rather than forcing the asker into one imperial language — is precisely the Pentecost move. Insisting that the network reflect the diversity of the Kingdom rather than the narrow slice nearest to its founders is precisely the Pentecost move. The shape of the coherence is the test of the coherence.

THE THESIS, W HOLE

Fragmentation is the wound. But not all coherence heals it. Babel-coherence — unified, self-naming, God-replacing — is worse than the wound. Pentecost-coherence — mutually intelligible, difference-honoring, God-proclaiming — is the healing. Movemental's refusals are the discipline that keeps the un-scattering on the Pentecost side of the line.

---

THE OPEN QUESTION

If the danger is real, then we owe ourselves a test we can actually apply. Good intentions and a list of refusals are not a test. If the difference between Pentecost and Babel is the difference between coherence that points away from itself and coherence that points at itself, then we should be able to say — concretely, observably — how we would know which one we had built by, say, the third year of the network's life. This paper does not yet answer that question. It insists the question be held open and answered honestly, and it offers the beginnings of where to look: In the fruit: are the people moving through the network being formed toward embodied local mission — actually planting, serving, discipling in their own places — or are they being formed into an audience that consumes the network and performs belonging to it? In the direction of attention: does the network continually point readers outward, toward their own contexts and toward God, or does it accumulate attention onto itself and its named figures? In the shape of the coherence: is the network becoming more mutually intelligible across genuine difference — more languages, more traditions, more economic and cultural distance — or is it converging toward a single recognizable house style and a narrowing in-group? In the posture of its center: do the most credible voices use their visibility to make others visible, or to consolidate it around themselves? These are not yet a finished instrument. They are the honest beginning of one. The discipline this paper asks for is that the test be built, applied, and revisited — because a movement that reverses fragmentation without watching the shape of its own coherence is a movement that has not understood why the tower fell.
