# Is There a Life Behind the Words? Authorship in the Age of AI

`momentofauthorship.pdf` · 3,265 words

AUTHORSHIP IN THE AGE OF AI

Is there a life behind the words? On what AI does to the trust between a writer and a reader — why the answer is not detection but disclosure, and what it would take for a reader to be able to trust an author again.

AUTHOR

Joshua Shepherd Founder, Movemental

Working theory Held for discussion

---

Start not with the writer, but with the faithful reader — who now faces something new in the history of the world. For the entire history of publishing, a faithful reader could assume that the work in front of them came from the person whose name was on it. That assumption has quietly ended, and almost no one has named what its ending costs. Consider a reader who has followed an author faithfully for years — read every book, taught the frameworks, been formed by the work. That reader opens the newest piece and, for the first time ever, cannot answer a question that never had to be asked before: is this still them? Is this the author, writing as they have always written? Is it the author mediated through an AI that changed the syntax, smoothed the edges, accelerated the draft? Is it the author's assistant and a model together? Is it the model, lightly supervised? Is it the model, entirely, wearing the author's name? The reader cannot tell. And the reader now knows enough about the glut of AI-produced content in the world to know that the question is real — which means even beginning to parse it carries a cost that reading never used to carry. This is the break. It is not about quality. A reader can love AI-assisted prose and still be wounded by not knowing whether a person stands behind it. The break is epistemic: the reader has lost the ability to know whether the words they are giving their attention and trust to were authored by the person they trust. No technology of the past — pen, press, typewriter, word processor — ever severed that link, because none of them could generate the thought. This one can. That is what is new.

---

When the uncertainty cannot be resolved, the reader does the only rational thing. They avoid. Faced with a question they cannot answer and a parsing cost they did not used to bear, the reader withdraws. Not out of cynicism — out of self-protection. If you cannot know whether the words in front of you were lived and meant by a person, and you cannot afford to keep paying the cost of wondering, the rational move is to stop giving your attention to work whose provenance you cannot establish. The product of unresolvable authorship uncertainty is avoidance. And avoidance has two faces, one of them good and one of them catastrophic. The good face: a reader who withdraws from uncertain publication may go back to their local, embodied community — to the pastor they can see, the teacher whose life they can watch, the friend whose word they can test against a life. That is a genuine good. If the AI glut sends people back to the people they can actually trust, something right is happening. The catastrophic face: publication itself is one of the primary means by which truth has been transmitted across distance and time for the whole history of the church and the world. The letter to a church the writer could not visit. The book that crossed an ocean. The treatise that outlived its author. If readers withdraw from publication wholesale because they can no longer trust its provenance, we do not merely lose some content. We lose the mechanism by which the body of Christ has thought together across space and generations. That is not a market problem. That is a civilizational one.

The danger is not that readers will be deceived by AI. The danger is that, unable to tell, they will stop trusting any written word they did not personally witness being lived — and publication, as a way of carrying truth, will quietly die of suspicion.

The steroid era and the live-ball era — the difference between the two is the whole argument. Baseball gives us two ways an era can change, and the difference between them is exactly the difference between the violation and the path forward.

THE STE RO ID E RA — THE UN DISCLO SE D

When Barry Bonds hit 73 home runs, the number could not mean what Roger Maris's 61 meant, because the achievement was no longer self-evidently human and no one had said so. The asterisk had to be imposed from outside, after the fact, because the player would not name what had changed. The achievement was real and also compromised — not because enhancement is unthinkable, but because it was hidden. This is the undisclosed AI author: output inflated, nothing said, trust broken in silence.

THE LIVE -BALL E RA — THE DISCLO SE D

In 1920, baseball changed the ball itself. Offense exploded. But there is no asterisk on the live-ball era, because the change was declared. Everyone knew the ball was different. The achievements still counted — they simply counted as achievements of a different, openly-named game. This is the path forward for AI and authorship: not a return to the dead-ball purity of pen and paper, but an openly disclosed change in how the work is made, so that the reader knows what game they are reading and can value it accurately.

PROVEN AN CE WITHOUT DETECTION

You do not need to examine the text. You need the author's own rhythm. Most of the AI-authorship conversation is stuck on detection at the level of the text — can we tell, from the words themselves, whether AI wrote this? That is a losing game and it gets more losing every month. There is a simpler move, available right now, while we wait to see whether technological verification ever arrives. You do not examine the sentences. You examine the rhythm. Just as you did not need a blood test to suspect that a player who went from ten home runs to fifty in a single season had changed something, you do not need semantic forensics to notice that an author who published ten books across a forty-year life has suddenly published ten in two years. The deviation from a person's own established baseline is the data. There is no plausible reason for a sixty-five-year-old writer to triple their lifetime output in a single year — and the author knows it better than anyone. If Alan looks at his own numbers and sees three times his lifetime rhythm in twelve months, Alan already knows that surplus is not coming out of who Alan is. This relocates the evidence from the place where AI is winning — the text — to the place where AI cannot hide: volume measured against a human life's established pace. THE BASE LIN E DO E S N O T CO N VICT. IT RE Q UE STS AN ACCO UN T.

There are honest reasons output rises — a sabbatical, a retirement, a long-gestating backlog, a coauthor. So the baseline is not a verdict. It is a prompt for disclosure. The deviation does not say "cheater." It says: this departs from your established rhythm; tell your readers why. The honest sabbatical author has a ready answer. The undisclosed inflater has none. The method does not accuse — it asks the author to give an account, and lets the presence or absence of an honest one speak. This is why it can only work as a system, not as an individual burden. One author trying to self-audit produces a mountain of unnecessary work. But a community that holds itself to this can show provenance at a glance.

The exact statistics will be refined, but the shape is the point: lifetime rhythm, current output against that rhythm, which jobs of writing AI assisted, whether AI did any generative authoring, the voice-fidelity standard the work is held to, and whether the author has published their process. Six facts. A reader can hold them in a glance, the way a fan reads a stat line, and decide whether to extend trust.

If the author self-reports their own pre-AI history, a cheater can game it. So the pre-AI baseline must be established from the public record — the bibliography that already exists, the publication dates already in the world, fixed before this moment and beyond retroactive editing. The network establishes the baseline from what is already public; the author discloses the present against it. The honesty of the system rests on the baseline being something the author cannot quietly rewrite.

The question was never "did AI touch this." It is "which job did AI do." The confusion about AI and authorship comes from treating "AI involvement" as a single dial from zero to a hundred. It was never one dial. Writing was always a bundle of distinct jobs, many of them already delegated — to assistants, editors, researchers, collaborators — for as long as there have been books. The honest question is not whether AI was involved, but which of writing's jobs it did. Some jobs were always delegable and raise no question of authorship when AI does them, provided it is disclosed: Remediation — moving existing work between forms, formats, and lengths. Research — gathering, locating, summarizing source material. Copy editing — grammar, consistency, mechanical correctness. These are the live-ball-era equivalents of better bats and better training. The game got faster; nothing was falsified. But there is a job that is different in kind: Generative authorship — the actual invention of the thought, the saying of the thing, the origination of the meaning. This is the home run itself. When AI performs this job, undisclosed, you are in steroid-era territory, regardless of how few or many words changed. And there is a genuinely new mode that the old categories cannot hold: collaborative authorship — a work co-created through back-and-forth conversation with a model, as a real and distinct act of writing. It is legitimate. It is also not the same act as solitary composition, and honesty about it costs something. As you might say of it plainly: this feat is less significant in some ways, and it is also its own kind of writing. The live ball still produced real achievements. It simply produced them in a declared, different game.

Output rate alone cannot tell acceptable acceleration from replacement — an author who used AI to research faster looks, in raw volume, like one who had AI write everything. So the statistics catch the undisclosed; the taxonomy of jobs defines the acceptable. The two need each other. Disclosure must say not only that output changed, but which job changed hands.

AN OLD DISCIPLIN E, EXTEN DED

The footnote is the precedent. Process becomes the new kind of source. A work made through deep collaboration with a model would best be supported by the cited, archived conversation from which it began. That sounds like an enormous new complexity — and it is — but it is not a new kind of demand. It is an old discipline catching up to a new tool. For five hundred years, serious writing has carried its sources in footnotes: here is the lineage of where this thought came from, so you can trace it and trust it. We already accept that careful work shows its sources. What collaborative AI authorship calls for is the same impulse extended to a new kind of source — not a footnote to prior books, but a trace back to the process: the conversation, the prompts, the human judgments that shaped what was generated. This paper does not solve that apparatus, and it should not try to yet. But naming the precedent changes how the complexity feels. Provenance is the new bibliography. The reader who wants to trust a collaboratively-authored work should be able to trace its process the way a scholar traces a citation. We are not inventing a strange new burden. We are extending the oldest discipline of trustworthy writing to the newest way of making it. And there is a grace clause that belongs here. AI has been broadly available for roughly three years. This is reasonably understood as a grace period for AI and authorship — a season in which the norms were genuinely unclear and many faithful people experimented without settled guidance. The call, therefore, is not to comb the past for offenders. It is to say: now is the moment to make your writing legible to your readers again, whatever you have done until now.

THE THEO LO GICAL CO RE

What we are really protecting is not human typing. It is the moment of authorship. Everything so far has been about honesty and trust. But underneath the honesty is a deeper claim about where authorship actually lives — and getting it right is what keeps this from being mere productivity ethics. We have always written with technology. The pen is a technology. So is paper, the typewriter, the word processor. Writing itself is a technological act. So the arrival of one more tool would be unremarkable — except that this tool is different in one specific and profound way: it can perform generative authorship. It can originate the thought, not merely transmit it. That is what makes the moment confusing, and it is also what forces us to ask a question we never had to ask before: what is the irreducible human act that makes a word trustworthy, regardless of the tool? The answer is not "a human typed it." A human can type every word and still produce dead text — words no one really meant, assembled out of habit or obligation, with no one truly present behind them. And a person can be wholly present, meaning every word, in a work co-created through conversation with a model. The tool is not the line. The line is something else:

THE K EY STONE

The moment of authorship is the moment a person, before God, means something — judges it, takes responsibility for it, and stands answerable for it as true. Words are trustworthy when that moment is present, and untrustworthy when it is absent, whether they came through pen, typewriter, or collaborative conversation with a model.

This reframes the entire system. Transparency is not the point. Transparency is the evidence that the moment of authorship happened. The statistics, the disclosure of jobs, the provenance of process — they are all proxies for one question: is there a person taking responsibility before God for this word? Was someone there, in the authorship, answerable for it? For a Christian author, this is not a productivity question; it is nearer to a question of bearing false witness. The authority of Christian writing has always been borrowed from a life — the claim that

this person has lived this, prayed this, suffered this, and is therefore worth heeding. The word is trustworthy because a life stands behind it. AI-inflated, undisclosed output severs the word from the life. It produces words no one lived behind, while implying that someone did. That is the deepest thing at stake: not output, not even honesty in the abstract, but whether there is a life behind the words — and whether the reader can be formed by what God actually spoke through a person in a real moment of authorship.

The point of making cheating visible is not to punish. It is to break the will to cheat. When the rhythm is visible to all, cheating becomes obvious — and when cheating becomes obvious, the will to cheat breaks, because the entire payoff of undisclosed AI inflation depends on no one being able to tell. Make provenance legible and you do not need an enforcement apparatus. You remove the incentive at its root. So the call, when a reader discovers that a trusted author has been silent about AI, is not to cancel. It is to invite repentance — which means simply: change what you are doing, and then tell your readers what you have learned. Given the genuine three-year grace period, almost everyone deserves the chance to realign rather than be destroyed. The right posture toward an author who has drifted is the oldest one the church knows: not exposure for its own sake, but a call to turn, and a way back to the table. This is the leader's actual job in this moment. Not to police, but to clarify the new reality and chart a clear way through it — to give writers and readers alike the frameworks to understand what writing always was, where AI can faithfully assist, what the genuinely new modes are, and how to make any of it legible to a human reader again. A community willing to hold itself to this can say to a skeptical reader: we say we do not do AI authorship, and here is how you can verify it — and mean it, in five or six statistics.

What is at stake is not productivity, and not even honesty in the abstract. It is whether the reader can trust the writer again — and trust the process through which the writing came to be.

That trust, once restored, lets the reader come back not merely as an audience but as someone who can again be formed by the work — formed by what God has spoken through a person in a real moment of authorship, which can happen through collaborative conversation with a model just as it has always happened through the older technologies of pen and page. The goal was never to keep AI out of writing. The goal is to keep the person — present, answerable, before God — in it.
