# Twenty Rules: Linking, Credibility, and Launch

`claude_network-rules-linking-credibility.md` · 2,023 words

### Twenty Rules

**For every leader building a platform in this network.**

*Linking, credibility, and launch — what to do and what not to do.*

---

#### Why any of this matters

Two things are happening to your work at once.

The first is that people find things differently now. A pastor with a question is as likely to ask an AI system as to search for it, and that system decides which sources to draw from and which to leave out. It makes that decision partly on what's on your site and largely on whether the rest of the internet corroborates you.

The second is that being alone online is now a real disadvantage. A single site — however good — is one more voice among millions. A group of leaders whose work genuinely connects, and whose connections are visible, reads as something different: a body of thought that more than one serious person stands behind.

You already have that offline. You cite each other, you speak at each other's events, you've written together, you send people to each other. The whole point of what follows is to make what is already true online too.

Every rule below serves one of two ends: **being believed**, or **being found**. Most serve both.

---

### The ten things to do

#### 1. Own your address

Buy your own domain, registered in your name, and keep it. Every link anyone ever makes to your work, every mention in a book or a podcast description, every time someone types your name into a phone — all of it accumulates against that one address, and it accumulates for years.

This is the one thing on this list that cannot be undone later. If your work lives at an address someone else controls, or at a temporary one, you are building on ground you can't keep.

#### 2. Make every profile of you point at the same place

Right now there are probably a dozen pages on the internet with your bio on them. Publishers, conference sites, networks you belong to, organizations you've founded, denominational directories. Most of them link somewhere stale or nowhere at all.

Go through them and get each one pointing at your site. It's about ten emails, each one two lines long: *could you update my link to this address?*

This does more than send traffic. It is how a machine confirms that the person on your site is the same person on your publisher's page and the same person in that conference program — that you are one real, verifiable human rather than a name on a website. If your name is shared with anyone else notable, this is the only thing that resolves the confusion.

#### 3. Link to another leader only where you actually build on their work

If your thinking genuinely extends someone else's — you use their framework, you're arguing with them, you learned it from them — link to it and say what the connection is. Those links are true, they help the reader, and they hold up under any scrutiny.

The test is simple: **would you make this link if search engines didn't exist?** If yes, make it. If the only reason is that you're both in the network, don't.

#### 4. Link with the actual name of the thing

Never "click here." Never a bare web address dropped in the middle of a sentence. Write the name of what you're pointing at: *Alan's work on the fivefold*, *Brad's covocational readiness questionnaire*, *the essay on theology of place*.

A reader learns what's on the other side before they click. So does a machine. This costs nothing and it's the difference between a link that carries meaning and one that carries none.

#### 5. Say out loud who you're connected to

Put the affiliations on the site. The network you're part of, the platform you build on, the organizations you work with, the people you've written with.

Hiding a relationship is what makes it look like something to hide. Every publisher group, every university system, every professional association states its connections plainly and nobody thinks worse of them for it. Stating yours converts a connection that looks arranged into one that looks ordinary — because it is.

#### 6. Put your own experience on the page

Not just what you think — what you've done. You planted the church. You lived in that neighborhood for eleven years. You trained those two hundred leaders. You made the mistake and here's what it cost.

This is the thing no one else can copy and no machine can generate. It's also, specifically, what search and AI systems are now built to look for: evidence that the person writing has actually been where they're writing about. Cite it plainly, in the first person, with details.

#### 7. Name your sources

When you make a claim, say where it came from. When you quote, give the book, the edition, and the page. When you use a statistic, link the study.

Two reasons. A reader trusts a writer who shows their work. And a machine deciding whether to draw on your page is looking for exactly this — claims that are anchored to something rather than floating.

#### 8. Publish under your own name, with your real bio and your real face

Every article carries your name, links to a full biography, and shows a photograph of you. The bio names your books, your roles, your training, your track record.

An anonymous page is a page nobody vouches for. A page with a named, verifiable author behind it is a page with a person's reputation attached. That's the whole distinction, and it's the cheapest one to get right.

#### 9. Keep your dates visible and refresh what goes stale

Show when a piece was published and when it was last updated. Then actually update the important ones once a year.

This matters more than most people realize. In controlled testing of how AI systems choose sources, a stale date was one of the few things that got a page ruled out almost entirely, regardless of how good it was. An undated page is treated as an old one.

#### 10. Get talked about somewhere other than your own site

Podcasts. Filmed conversations. Guest pieces on other people's sites. Interviews. Conference sessions that get posted publicly. Your name in someone else's article.

This is the single strongest predictor anyone has found of whether AI systems will draw on your work — stronger than anything you can do on your own pages, and much stronger than links. A new site with nothing said about it anywhere else is invisible no matter how well it's built. Being discussed in places that are already established is what gets you admitted.

Video especially. It's the highest correlation in the available research and almost nobody in our world is doing it on purpose.

### The ten things not to do

#### 1. Don't link to another leader just because they're in the network

A link between two people who genuinely build on each other is evidence. A link between two people who've never engaged each other's work is decoration, and at scale it stops looking like decoration and starts looking like a scheme.

The network's whole advantage is that our connections are real. Manufacture a few and you've traded the one thing that can't be copied for something anyone can fake.

#### 2. Don't run two sites for yourself

If you have an older site — a blog, a ministry site, an assessment tool — don't leave it running alongside the new one. Two sites in your name compete with each other, split every link you've ever earned, and leave every system that encounters you unsure which one is actually you.

Pick one address. Point the other one at it permanently. A decade of accumulated credibility moves in a single afternoon, and if you skip it, that decade goes on working against you instead of for you.

#### 3. Don't buy links, trade links, or put keyword phrases in press release links

Paying for a link, swapping links as a favor, or sending out an announcement stuffed with links that use your target search phrase as the clickable text — all three are explicitly named as manipulation by Google, and press releases are called out by name.

If a link is paid or placed, it gets marked as such. This is a solved problem with a technical answer, and your web person knows it. Just don't let anyone talk you into the shortcut.

#### 4. Don't hide who you're connected to

The mirror of rule five. An undisclosed relationship discovered later costs you more than a disclosed one ever could. Say it plainly and it's context. Let someone find it and it's a story.

#### 5. Don't send email to a list that isn't yours

If an organization has an audience you'd like to reach, ask them to send your note to it. Do not ask them to export it. Those people subscribed to that organization, not to you, and mailing them from an address they don't recognize damages your ability to reach anyone's inbox for months — and costs you the relationship on top.

The organization sends, or nobody sends.

#### 6. Don't ask your people for three things at once

Read this, subscribe to that, share this, buy the book, join the cohort. Every one of those is reasonable and all of them together are noise.

One clear invitation. You get a limited number of moments when the people who trust you will act on your word, and the way to waste them is to spend several at once on a crowded ask.

#### 7. Don't block the AI systems from reading your site

There's a file on every website that tells automated readers what they may and may not look at. It's easy to set it defensively, and some platforms do it by default. If yours blocks the AI crawlers, you have removed yourself from the systems that decide what gets recommended — and you won't notice, because nothing breaks. You just quietly aren't there.

Ask whoever built your site to confirm the AI readers are allowed. It takes them two minutes.

#### 8. Don't claim the network is bigger than it is

Describe what's actually true. Two colleagues whose work connects is interesting and verifiable. "A movement of leaders" said over two websites is a claim your audience will check.

Our readers are unusually good at spotting the gap between what's promised and what's on the shelf. Under-claim and let the thing grow into its description.

#### 9. Don't publish volume for its own sake

Twenty thin pages written quickly to cover more search terms will do less than four pieces that genuinely say something. Search and AI systems are now explicitly built to detect and discard exactly that pattern, and your actual readers detect it faster.

Publish when you have something. The one exception is that dormancy has a cost too — see the next one.

#### 10. Don't let launch be the loudest week you ever have

The most common failure is not a bad launch. It's a good launch followed by silence.

Everything about how this works rewards accumulation. Sites that matter are years old. Audiences are built by showing up repeatedly. A single burst tells everyone watching that this was a project. A steady rhythm — even a modest one — tells them it's a place.

Before you announce anything, decide what you're publishing in week four, week eight, and week twelve, and write the dates down. Month two should be bigger than month one.

---

#### The one-sentence version

**Make the true things visible, say the connections out loud, get talked about somewhere other than your own site, and keep showing up.**

Everything above is a footnote to that.
