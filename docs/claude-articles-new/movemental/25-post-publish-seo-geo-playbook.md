GEO in the real world

### What GEO does — and what happens when everyone does it.

Classic SEO earns a **click** : your link appears and a person chooses it. GEO earns something different — a place **inside the answer**. When someone asks ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini about your topic, the model writes the reply and may lean on you, name you, and point to you. The first reader is the model; the human reads what the model made of you.

#### For your work, on its own

Optimizing for GEO means the model can **reach** your work (its crawlers get in), **lift** it cleanly (clear claims, statistics, definitions it can quote), **trust** it (a recognized author, corroborated across the web), and find it **current**. The prediction is concrete: answers about your topic start to carry your framing and your name, instead of a competitor's or none at all.

#### Now assume a hundred credible leaders run the same playbook

This is the real world, and it changes the story. Everyone in your field reads the same guidance and optimizes the same way. Five things follow.

  1. **It becomes the admission ticket, not an edge.** When every credible voice is extractable and crawlable, doing it no longer sets you apart — it only qualifies you to be considered. What changes most is the **penalty for absence** : the leader who skips it isn't merely behind, they're structurally excluded from the answer, invisible no matter how good the ideas.
  2. **The answer still names only a few.** A model builds each reply from a handful of sources, not a hundred. Among a field of optimized peers it has to **choose** , and the tie-breakers are no longer the mechanics everyone shares — they're authority, distinctiveness, corroboration, and freshness.
  3. **It resolves into a power law.** A small number of voices become the ones the models name again and again; the rest sit in the training data, present but rarely surfaced. Optimization gets you into the pool; it does not spread attention evenly across it.
  4. **Sameness dissolves into the average.** When a hundred people optimize identically, their content converges — same structures, same phrasings, same points — and the model blends it into a generic consensus with no one attached. What survives that blending is a **proprietary framework, original data, or a nameable position**. Distinctiveness is the only thing that comes out the other side with your name on it.
  5. **Being the origin others build on is the strongest place to stand.** If the other ninety-nine use your language — if the field argues in terms you coined — the model treats you as the source even while paraphrasing them. Owning the vocabulary of the conversation beats out-optimizing it.

Illustrative: across an optimized field, share of AI mentions concentrates on a few named voices while the rest stay in the data, rarely surfaced.

#### What to predict from ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini

Two doors lead into an AI answer, and GEO feeds both. **Training data** favors volume and long-standing, widely-referenced authority — who the web has treated as important for years. **Live retrieval** favors what is fresh, crawlable, well-structured, and currently trusted. The models differ in how much they lean on each — a retrieval-heavy assistant leans on the live web, a training-heavy one on what it already absorbed — but the levers are identical, so the same body of work that ranks also gets cited. As a whole field optimizes, each model's answer on the topic settles into a **stable consensus** dominated by the few most-authoritative and most-distinctive voices, refreshed by whoever published most recently.

#### The honest takeaway

GEO is necessary but not sufficient. Doing it moves you from **excluded to eligible** ; it does not, by itself, move you from eligible to named. In a crowded field the durable advantage isn't the optimization mechanics everyone shares — it's owning the ideas the field uses and being the most-corroborated origin of them. Optimize to qualify; differentiate and earn authority to win. For a body of work built on named frameworks, that origin position is the whole game.

The scenius effect

### What happens when the whole network cross-links, over time.

The competition story above assumes rivals. The more important story is **cooperation** : a field of credible leaders who deliberately link to and cite each other's work, meaningfully, over years. That isn't a hundred sites splitting a pie — it's a **scenius** , a scene whose collective genius compounds a shared position. It changes the prediction, because authority and citation are properties of a **network** , not of a page. One optimized site is a node shouting into the void; a hundred interlinked ones are a graph, and both Google and the AI models read that graph as the definition of who matters in the field.

Illustrative: a densely cross-linked network reads to search and AI as one authority hub — with an origin node (green) others build on.

#### In search

A backlink is worth more when it comes from a high-authority site. When every leader is individually well-optimized **and** they link to each other's pillars, every inbound link arrives from a strong domain — so the same number of links carries far more weight. Authority recirculates: each site lifts the others, which raises the value of the links they send, which lifts the others again. It's a feedback loop, not a sum. The cluster starts out-ranking outsiders on the field's terms, and a rising tide lifts the smaller nodes — a newer leader inside the network inherits credibility they could never earn alone.

#### In AI answers

Models infer who is authoritative in a domain largely from how often sources **co-occur and reference each other**. When the leaders cite each other's frameworks and name each other, they manufacture exactly that corroboration signal: these names keep appearing together as the authorities here. The models learn the **cluster** as the answer to the topic — so instead of one winner taking most, several are named together as a recognized school of thought. And because each leader owns a distinct sub-territory and points to the others for theirs, the network covers the whole subject with no internal cannibalization: every question in the field hands off to someone in the cluster, and every hand-off is another corroborating link. Comprehensive and non-redundant is precisely what a model wants to cite.

#### Over time

This compounds on a curve, not a line. In year one the cross-links exist but carry little weight. As each node earns its own outside authority and the internal links mature, the graph's total authority rises — and because it recirculates, growth accelerates before it saturates. An outsider arriving three years later doesn't face a hundred separate competitors; they face one established, interlinked, model-recognized institution. The network becomes the incumbent, and the barrier it builds is far higher than the sum of its members.

#### The one rule that makes it real

The links have to be **meaningful and editorial** — genuine intellectual references between people who actually build on each other's work — not a reciprocal link scheme on a schedule. Google has spent twenty years killing link farms, and a mechanical exchange gets flagged; real cross-citation is the strongest authority signal there is, because it is indistinguishable from the organic thing it actually is. The strategy is to make the real relationships legible to the crawlers, not to fabricate relationships that aren't there.

Corrected prediction: if the network cooperates instead of competes, you don't get a power law where a few win and the rest vanish. You get a **compounding authority commons** — a rising floor that lifts every genuine member, a shared position as _the_ recognized school on the topic in both search results and AI answers, and a moat that widens over time. Modeling a lone platform systematically undersells what's possible, because credibility is a graph — and a graph can be grown on purpose.

After creation

### The publication layer — agent drafts, human approves.

Everything after "create" runs as a pipeline that produces drafts a human signs off. Eight components make it real:

  1. **The templating engine (the invisible layer).** Emits every derived and copied field automatically on render — canonical, robots/snippet directives, Open Graph + Twitter, the type-aware JSON-LD skeleton, sitemap, breadcrumbs, image sizing, plus per-type emitters (Scholar citation_*, podcast RSS itunes:*, video VTT + SeekToAction). Built once, runs on every publish — the lift that collapses per-post work to a handful of fields.
  2. **The publish pipeline.** On publish the agent recipes run in order, each a reviewable card — title/meta, schema fill, FAQ, alt + share image, internal + network links, post-publish QA, distribution, plus transcript and DOI/RSS prep — with approve / edit / regenerate.
  3. **Human-in-the-loop review.** The pipeline is a stack of cards; voice-bearing outputs carry an Alan-ratification gate; refreshes show as a diff.
  4. **Gates as hard blocks.** Publish is blocked when a gate fails: not indexable, no transcript, no DOI, archive not public, Core Web Vitals failing.
  5. **Credential vault + integrations.** Search Console, Crossref/DataCite, ORCID, the podcast host/RSS, YouTube, a social scheduler, the newsletter platform — the agent acts through them; the human supplies credentials and approves every send, submit, and deposit.
  6. **The link-graph service.** Maintains the internal corpus graph and the peer-network graph so link proposals (internal + network) can be computed.
  7. **The monitoring / refresh loop.** Watches rankings, snippet and AI-citation capture, backlinks and decay, drafting refreshes for approval.
  8. **The provenance registry.** Every claim resolves to a source; integrity enforced at creation, surfaced at publish.

Derived and copied work is invisible and automatic; agent-authored work is a review card; earned work is tracked and drafted but human-executed. The human reviews and ratifies — they don't hand-build tags.

The whole system

### What the dashboard needs beyond a single piece.

The editor and pipeline handle a piece; the dashboard manages the system — a compounding graph. Ten layers:

  1. **Corpus / content-graph view** — every piece by tier, its internal links, orphans flagged, and a coverage map of the field's queries.
  2. **Scores view** — each piece's SEO and GEO score, its banked / authored / earned split, gate status, and reach-at-publish vs earned-over-time, rolled up to a platform authority trend.
  3. **Network / scenius view** — the peer-leader graph, where genuine overlaps exist, a framework-attribution map, outreach status, and the cluster's compounding-authority trend.
  4. **Query-coverage & gap map** — the field's query space with volume, competition, and where to publish next; feeds the brief agent.
  5. **AI-citation tracking** — the GEO analog of rank tracking: scheduled prompts across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity, logging when and how you get cited.
  6. **Framework / entity registry** — Alan's frameworks as first-class entities linked across corpus, schema and the attribution map: the origin-node strategy, operationalized.
  7. **Content health / decay** — freshness cadence, ranking and citation tracking, the refresh queue.
  8. **Production kanban** — everything in flight by state: drafting, in review, awaiting ratification, awaiting credentials, published, needs refresh.
  9. **Provenance registry** — the central claim-to-source store, shared by every editor.
  10. **Roles & ratification** — editor, producer, Alan (ratifier), the network-relationship owner, and the states that route work between them.

The value was never in one optimized page; it was in a system that grows its own authority on purpose. Build-readiness note: the templating engine and AI-citation tracking are the heaviest, least off-the-shelf pieces — the templating engine makes everything else cheap (build it first); AI-citation tracking is an immature category (treat it as a rough signal, not a precise dial).

How to read this +

**The points are an attribution, not a promise.** Each number answers: in a fully, well-executed playbook for this content type, what share of the realized credibility does this task account for? It is not "do this and get X% more traffic."

**Gates, levers, and differentiators behave differently.** Gates (foundation, performance) are pass/fail — once met they add little, but if failed they cap everything above them (toggle them to see). Levers (content, authority) hold most of the 100 points. Differentiators (schema, snippet formatting, Core Web Vitals) are small alone but decide close races.

**SEO and GEO reward the same work on different curves.** SEO rewards _rankings_ , so authority and content depth dominate. GEO rewards _citation frequency_ — being one of the few sources an answer weaves in across many prompts — so it reweights toward extractable structure, statistics, quotations, entity trust, presence on AI-trusted sources, and freshness.

**How the layers compound:** foundation makes a page _eligible_ ; content makes it _worth surfacing_ ; authority makes it _trusted enough to rank or be cited_ ; structure and schema make it _extractable_ ; distribution creates the engagement and link velocity that feed back into authority and citation. Remove one layer and the ones above it lose their footing.

**Value per edit — build-once, per-post, earned.** The 100 points don't cost the same effort. Some are **build-once** : the invisible tag, schema and performance layer you template a single time and every post inherits for free. Some are **per-post authored** : the content itself and the few fields that need human judgment. Some are **earned** off-platform over weeks — links, reviews, citations, trusted-source presence. ## What a score of 100 actually means.

The number is a model, not a dial an engine reads. No search engine or answer engine assigns your post a 100. The scale asks one honest question about a single piece of content: have you done everything on your side of the line, and has the world done its part yet?

### What 100 is not

It isn't a ranking, and it isn't traffic. You can execute a post perfectly and still sit below a stronger domain, or lose a citation to a source a model already trusts more. Ranking and citation are competitive outcomes that depend on everyone else and on the exact query. The score measures the one thing you control: how complete, legible and credible the post is. It's a readiness ceiling, not a scoreboard.

### What 100 is

A post at 100 is the best possible version of itself on every axis you can influence — crawlable and indexed, saying something worth surfacing, structured so a human, a search engine and an answer engine can all lift the point, carrying the right invisible tags and schema, loading well, showing who wrote it and why they can be trusted, and having earned the links, reviews and citations that tell the world it's real. When all of that is true it stops being a page you hope someone finds and becomes an asset that keeps paying.

### Why you can't reach 100 on publish day

The points come from three places. Some are **banked** — the invisible tag-and-schema layer and the performance work you build once as templates, inherited free by every post. Some are **authored** — the actual content, written fresh each time. And some are **earned** — the backlinks, reviews, citations and trusted-source presence that aren't yours to type and accrue over weeks as the world vouches for the post.

So a flawlessly executed post is only about two-thirds of the way to 100 the day you publish. You own the banked points — automation delivered them the moment the page rendered — and the authored points, which you wrote. The last third is a promissory note the post cashes over time. Reaching 100 is a later event, if it happens at all: the moment the post is both perfectly made and fully validated by the world. A post living steadily in the eighties is doing its job.

### What this means for your time

This is the whole reason to separate the buckets. The banked layer — the part that used to eat hours per post in tools like Yoast — is deterministic. Build it once and it costs nothing again; a publisher should never touch a canonical tag or a schema skeleton by hand. That frees every remaining hour for the only two things that move the points automation can't: writing content worth citing, and doing the slow relational work that earns authority. Automation doesn't raise your ceiling — it stops the ceiling from stealing the time you need to reach it.
