# The Plan, Told Straight: Movemental Strategy

`movemental-the-plan-told-straight.md` · 3,031 words

### The Plan, Told Straight

*An outside read on how Movemental gets from one English network to six linked ones — the story before we put numbers to it. Written the way a YC partner or an HBR case writer would work through it: honestly, step by step, and stopping at every point where a number has to be a judgment call rather than a fact.*

---

#### First, what kind of company this actually is

Start where an outside investor would, because it's the thing that's easy to miss from inside the building. Movemental looks like a Christian formation network. It isn't, or not mainly. It's a high-margin AI platform company that has quietly solved the single hardest problem those companies have: getting trusted access to buyers.

Most AI shops spend their lives buying attention. They run ads, they cold-email, they discount, and they still fight to be believed, because nobody trusts a vendor they've never heard of to touch the work that matters. Movemental doesn't have that problem. A hundred respected people vouch for it. Their names are the reason a seminary or a church network picks up the phone. That's the whole game, and it should be said plainly before we walk the plan: **the network isn't the product, it's the distribution — and it's the kind of distribution money usually can't buy.**

There are two engines here, and they do completely different jobs. Confusing them is the fastest way to misread the business.

The first is the **leaders' engine** — the movement leaders and everything they sell to their own audiences: courses, books, subscriptions, events. Movemental keeps only a thin slice of this (10%), and that's on purpose. This engine's job isn't to make Movemental money. Its job is to earn trust and reach, and to give each leader a reason to bring their audience and their organizational relationships into one place. It's the credibility. It's the front door.

The second is the **tech engine** — custom platform builds and the recurring agent management behind them, sold to organizations. This is where the money is actually made. A build takes about a week, costs almost nothing to produce, and runs at roughly 90% margin. The leaders' engine fills the room with warm, credible buyers; the tech engine sells them the thing that pays.

Hold those two apart and the rest of the plan reads clearly. The leaders are the reach and the moat. The tech is the margin. Everything that follows is about multiplying both.

---

#### Where we actually are — the proven core

It's month three, and the core is already real, not theoretical. There's a first organization in the work (Youthfront). There are founding leaders (Alan, Brad, and the early cohort). The cheap, urgent wedge is live: the Safety charter that says *you're exposed without an AI policy, and we'll give you one for a thousand dollars*. And there's a genuine pipeline behind it — a seminary, a forty-church network, several nonprofits, an agency, a path into library networks through a fundraising partner.

So the first claim in the story is modest and well-supported: **the proven core is an English leader network plus a near-pure-profit tech engine, and it works.** Even if nothing else in this plan ever happened, that's a real, valuable company. Everything past this point is about how far it can be pushed — and the honest analyst's job is to separate the part that's close to certain from the part that's a bet.

---

#### The first move: fill English to 100, then lock the door

The plan says: get to 100 English-speaking movement leaders, then close the network to new individual English leaders. On the surface that looks like leaving money on the table. It's the opposite, for three reasons that build on each other.

**A closed network is worth more than an open one.** The entire value of the thing is trust. An open door means the hundred-and-first leader, and the two-hundredth, and eventually the network stops meaning anything because anyone can be in it. Curation *is* the product. Locking the door at a round, defensible number turns membership into a status good — something leaders want to be inside, and stay inside, precisely because not everyone can be. *Therefore* the network's credibility holds instead of eroding as it grows.

**A full network is a complete distribution machine.** A hundred leaders, each with an audience and each with organizational relationships — seminaries they teach at, denominations they advise, church networks they've planted — is a warm-referral engine pointed straight at the tech engine. *Therefore* the platform business gets its pipeline handed to it, which is exactly what we see already: Tampa Underground comes in warm and discounted precisely because it's leader-affiliated.

**Locking English frees the team to do the next thing.** You can't run the same recruiting play forever. Declaring English "done" at 100 is what lets the team stop selling to individual English leaders and start building the next network. *Therefore* the door-lock isn't a limit; it's the trigger for the growth move that follows.

One honest note here, because it matters for the numbers later: locking the door also lets you *curate for reach*. A closed network of a hundred can be assembled to skew toward leaders with real audiences, rather than filling the last slots with whoever's available. That's why, when we get to the projection, I'd argue for a blended revenue-per-leader closer to the top of the range than the middle. (More on that number below — it's a judgment call, and I'll defend it as one.)

---

#### The real growth move: run the whole play again, in five more languages

Here's the part the earlier work hadn't accounted for, and it's the part that turns a good company into a large one.

Once English is full and the playbook is proven, replicate the entire model — the scenius, the formation stack, the books, the subscriptions, the tech engine — in each of the other languages: Latin American Spanish, Brazilian Portuguese, Korean, German, and Mandarin. Recruit native-speaker movement leaders in each. Link every language network to the English one and to each other. Run the same playbook. And use a mix of the existing network's pull, referrals, and paid ads to get each new one off the ground.

Walk the logic, because it's a clean chain and each link lowers the cost or raises the payoff of the next.

**The content already exists.** The translation work in the earlier plan means the courses, books, and audiobooks are already in these languages before a single leader is recruited. *Therefore* a language network launches with a full catalog on day one — which is nothing like the cold start English had, where the content was built as the network grew. The hardest, slowest part is already done.

**The playbook is proven.** By the time you launch Spanish, you've run the English network end to end — you know the pricing, the conversion, the wedge, the build motion, the recurring line. *Therefore* you're not experimenting in the new language; you're repeating a known routine. The risk in each language is execution, not invention.

**Native leaders self-seed the network.** The whole reason the English network grows without a sales budget is cross-recommendation — leaders vouching for leaders. The moment you have a few trusted anchor leaders in Spanish, that same free engine starts turning in Spanish. *Therefore* paid ads are only needed to jump-start each network — to buy the first audience and help land the first anchors — not to sustain it. That's a crucial distinction for the cost side: ads are a match, not a fuel line.

**Each native leader brings their region's organizations.** This is the link people forget. A Korean movement leader doesn't just bring a Korean audience; they bring Korean seminaries, churches, and nonprofits — which means the tech engine, the part that actually makes money, now has a warm pipeline in Korea. *Therefore* replicating the network in a language isn't only replicating content revenue. It's replicating the near-pure-profit platform business in a whole new region.

**And the tech engine doesn't care what language it's in.** A platform build is a build; agent management is agent management. The economics are the same in São Paulo as in Kansas City. *Therefore* the multiplier isn't on the small, leader-favorable content lines — it's on the biggest, highest-margin engine in the company. That's the point of the whole move.

Put the chain together and the five-year picture stops being "the English network, plus some translation revenue." It becomes **six linked trust-networks, each running the full stack, all feeding one shared, language-agnostic, high-margin platform business.**

#### Every revenue stream, and where it shows up in this bigger picture

To make sure nothing goes missing, here is the full stack — and the plan runs it, in time, in each language-region, not just in English:

- **Formation** — cohort courses, self-paced paths, curriculum bundles, the app, and the turnkey church *curriculum* license. The reach layer. Mostly the leaders' money (they keep 90%); Movemental's slice is thin but the credibility it buys is the whole point.
- **Books** — direct digital sales with agent tutors, the search-and-AI discovery that sells them as a byproduct, Amazon editions (no Movemental take, pure goodwill and reach), AI audiobooks, book-and-agent pairings, curriculum bundles, and print through a publisher partner (100 Movements in English; other languages would need their own print partners, which is a real to-do, not a given).
- **Subscriptions and monthly support** — direct, per-leader, sovereign. The list is the asset; the network's cross-recommendation is the cheapest subscriber acquisition in the model. No pooled all-access product — shared discovery, sovereign monetization.
- **Events** — leader workshops, brokered speaking, and a regional gathering per network.
- **The tech engine** — the Safety wedge, the Sandbox/Training/Solutions methodology, the custom platform builds, the network conversions (a Tampa in every region), and the recurring agent-management license. This is the money, and it's the stream the multilingual move multiplies most.
- **Shared-value leader comp** — the 10% referral and the paid consultation seat — flowing to leaders on the tech business they bring in, in every language.

And two effects that only exist *because* the networks are linked: **cross-recommendation across languages** (a reader of a Spanish leader discovering the English catalog, and the reverse), and **one shared platform and brand**, so the cost of running network number six is far below the cost of running number one.

#### Now the honest part: what's certain, what's a bet, and what could kill it

A good outside read doesn't just narrate the upside. Here's where I'd push.

**The English core is close to a sure thing.** Proven pipeline, proven wedge, a product that costs a week to build at 90% margin, and a referral network that lowers customer acquisition to near zero. If the multilingual play never happened, this is still a company worth building. That's the floor, and it's a high one.

**The multilingual replication is a real bet, and it's where the execution risk concentrates.** Three risks matter, in order:

*First, native operators and trust.* The entire moat is credibility, and credibility is local. You cannot run a Korean or a Brazilian network convincingly from Kansas City. Each language needs trusted native anchor leaders — and probably local staff — and a weak or wrong anchor doesn't just underperform, it poisons the trust the whole network runs on. This is the risk I'd watch hardest. It's a people problem, not a translation problem, and it's the one that can't be solved with a good build.

*Second, ability to pay.* English, German, and Korean audiences and organizations can pay real money. Spanish and Portuguese markets are enormous but monetize well below that. Mandarin is the extreme case — huge reach, and by your own redemptive stance, largely given away. So the revenue-per-leader is not uniform across languages, and the grant-funded dynamic that makes the U.S. tech sale so easy may not exist the same way abroad. The multilingual play multiplies reach cleanly; it multiplies *revenue* unevenly, and the model has to say so honestly.

*Third, bandwidth.* A small team running six networks, a book operation, a subscription operation, and a build shop will hit a wall unless the whole thing is genuinely productized and locally staffed. Growth that outruns the team's ability to hold quality is how trust-based businesses die.

**What makes the bet fundable rather than reckless is the asymmetry.** You are not launching cold. The content is already translated, the playbook is proven, the product is built, and the English network gives each new language a warm start. So the marginal cost of language number two is a fraction of what English cost — and that gap is the actual argument for doing it. This is a bet where the downside is bounded and the upside is a whole new region's worth of the highest-margin business you have.

**And the discipline that makes it sane is sequencing.** Don't light all five at once. Prove Spanish first — it's the largest market and the closest culturally to the U.S. work. If Spanish works, the thesis is validated and the rest is repetition. If Spanish struggles, you've learned it cheaply, before betting the team on five. That single sequencing rule is the difference between a confident expansion and an overreach, and I'd hold the team to it.

#### The judgment calls, named — so we can argue about them before we chart them

You asked me to be clear about every number I'd have to invent. Here they are, each with the reasoning, so when we build the projection we're stress-testing stated beliefs and not hidden ones. (The conservative/base/aggressive scale comes later; this is just me showing my work.)

**Blended revenue per English leader: I'd argue ~$90k, not $85k.** The worked example of a mid-sized leader lands near $92k. My earlier models pulled the blend down to $85k to account for a long tail of smaller leaders. But *locking the door lets you curate for reach* — a closed hundred can be assembled to skew larger than an open one would. So I'd nudge the blend up to about $90k and defend it on the curation logic. The tail still exists; it's just shorter in a network you get to choose.

**Leaders per language network: not a flat 100.** Market size and the size of the *monetizable* audience differ. I'd carry roughly: English 100 (locked), Spanish ~100 (huge market), Portuguese ~75, Korean ~50, German ~40, Mandarin ~60 (large audience, constrained monetization). These are sized to real audience and real ability to pay, not to symmetry.

**Revenue per leader by language, as a share of English.** English is the baseline. German I'd actually put slightly *above* (~105%) — small network, but high willingness to pay. Korean ~90%. Spanish and Portuguese ~55% — big audiences, lower ability to pay. Mandarin ~30% — mostly redemptive by design. The reasoning is ability-to-pay and market maturity, and it's the honest counterweight to the "just multiply by six" temptation.

**Tech-engine strength by region.** Highest in the U.S./English, where the grant ecosystem, the agency market, and your home-field build advantage all sit. I'd run the other regions at roughly 40–70% of the English tech rate, ramping, because grant funding and the agency-services market look different abroad and your delivery starts U.S.-based. This is a guess, clearly labeled — it's the variable I'd most want real data on early.

**Paid acquisition cost.** A real, new line item that English never needed. English grew on founding leaders and network gravity; a new language starts cold, so you buy the first audience and help land the first anchors, then let native cross-recommendation take over. I'd model it as a jump-start cost per language, not an ongoing one — and flag that the true number is unknown until you run the first Spanish campaigns.

**Timing.** English fills Years 1–2. Spanish launches Year 2 (largest, closest, content ready). Portuguese and Korean in Year 3. German and Mandarin in Year 4. Staggered on purpose, per the sequencing discipline above — prove the replication once before betting on five.

**Native operators as a cost and a risk, not a rounding error.** Each language needs trusted local anchors and likely local staff. I'll carry that as real cost and real risk in the model, because pretending it's free is exactly how this kind of plan flatters itself.

---

#### So what — why the plan holds together

Step back and the shape is clean. This is not a bigger content network. It's a **federation of trust-networks feeding one high-margin platform business.** The leaders are the distribution and the moat. The tech is the money. The languages are the multiplier. And the reason the plan makes sense is that each piece pays for the next: the English network proves the model and funds the ads; the ads and the cross-recommendation seed the language networks; the language networks bring native leaders whose organizations feed the tech engine in six regions; and the tech engine — near-pure-profit and indifferent to language — turns all of that reach into margin.

The bottom line an outside partner would give you: **the English core is the sure thing, and the multilingual replication is a genuine, ordered, fundable bet with a clear kill-switch in Spanish.** That combination — already good on the proven part, with a disciplined path to being large — is rare. Most companies have one or the other. The main thing that turns this story into the projection we're about to build is honest numbers on the parts that are still guesses: the per-leader revenue by language, the tech-engine strength abroad, the cost to acquire the first audience in each new tongue, and the team's true capacity to hold quality across six networks at once.

Get those four honest, and the rest of the story does what it says it will.
