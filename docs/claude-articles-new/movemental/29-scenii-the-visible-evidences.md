# Scenii: The Visible Evidences

### Scenii — The Visible Evidences

*Operational reference: what makes a Scenii structurally distinguishable from a federation of separate websites. Walks through, layer by layer, what someone actually sees in the UI and the dashboard. None of the items below are achievable by an individual freelancer building five separate sites; each requires the other four nodes to exist and to have agreed to the architecture. Together, these items are the visible answer to "what makes this an ecosystem rather than a collection of platforms."*

*Worked example throughout: an authority network for the Christian foster-care movement, with five nodes — Cause-Centered Hub (CAFO), Theological Voice, Practitioner Implementation, Church Mobilization, Storytelling/Narrative.*

---

#### On the public-facing front end

##### Cross-node citation chrome

Every long-form article on the theological-voice site that references foster-care field practice carries a visible *Cited in field by:* strip — small avatars and org names linking to the specific practitioner article that operationalizes the theological claim. The same article shows *Mobilized through:* with church-network resources that translated it into congregational practice, and *Witnessed in:* with stories that exemplify it. Three reciprocal strips per substantive article. The chrome is automatic, populated by the shared ontology, and shows readers — and AI crawlers — that this is not a lone essay but a node in a corroborated graph.

##### Shared glossary tooltips

When the practitioner site uses a term like *trauma-informed care* or *family preservation*, the term is underlined with a faint marker. Hovering reveals a tooltip definition sourced from the hub's canonical glossary, with a "defined by CAFO" attribution and links to where the term is used across the other four nodes. The same tooltip appears on every node. Vocabulary is no longer per-org; it is movement-wide. A reader on any node sees that the entire ecosystem speaks one language with one source of canonical definition.

##### Movement-wide federated search

A single search box, present on every node, that returns results from all five sites simultaneously, faceted by node type. Search "trauma-informed sermon prep" and you see theological frames from the visionary voice, training modules from the practitioner, sermon kits from the church network, family testimonies from the storytelling layer, and the canonical definition from the hub. No reader has to know which org publishes what. The movement appears as one knowledge surface.

##### Cross-node author bylines

A practitioner article co-authored with a theologian shows both bylines, with both authors' canonical bios pulled live from their home nodes, and "Also writes at:" footer chips. When the same theologian publishes solo on the theological-voice site, that article shows "Practiced in the field by:" links to the practitioner's training modules. Authorship becomes graph-aware. Readers see that the field's intellectual leaders are not isolated bloggers but interconnected contributors with traceable practice partners.

##### Coordinated taxonomy pages

Visit `/topics/foster-family-preservation` on any of the five sites, and you see the same canonical topic page — same definition, same featured resources curated from across the ecosystem, same suggested learning pathway — but rendered in the host org's chrome and accent color, with that org's relevant work surfaced first. The topic exists once, semantically; the rendering happens per-node. New topics added to the hub appear automatically on the peer sites within hours.

##### The "movement view" toggle

A small persistent UI element on every site — a five-circle pictogram in the header — that lets a reader toggle from this-site-only view to whole-ecosystem view. Whole-ecosystem view re-renders the homepage, search results, and topic pages with content from all five nodes interleaved by relevance rather than source. Readers can read the whole movement as if it were one publication, while authors retain their independent identities.

##### Visible learning pathways that span the network

A church considering foster-care ministry visits the church-mobilization site and finds a pathway titled *Becoming a foster-care-mobilizing church.* The pathway has twelve modules. Module one is a theological frame from the visionary voice. Module two is a practitioner's training piece. Module three is a story from a family. Module four is a board-conversation guide from the church-mobilization node itself. The reader walks through what would have been four separate websites as one coherent journey, with progress saved, completion certified, and the path itself credentialed across the network.

##### Cross-org event surfaces

Every node carries the ecosystem's shared events calendar in addition to its own. A conference hosted by CAFO surfaces on the practitioner site under "movement events," with curated context for practitioners; on the church-mobilization site with context for pastors; on the theological-voice site with the relevant intellectual track highlighted. One event, five contextually-curated entry points.

##### Shared press and reference kits

Journalists writing about Christian foster care can land on any of the five nodes and find a *Press & reference* page that includes the canonical statistics from the hub, the theological framing from the visionary voice, named practitioners available for interview, church-mobilization spokespeople, and curated family stories with release permissions. One movement, one credible front door for the press, accessible from anywhere.

##### The visible "movement footer"

Every node carries a unified footer strip — small node-org logos linking to each peer, a one-line description of how each fits, and an "About this network" link that explains the Scenii model. A reader who lands anywhere in the network sees, within one scroll, that they are inside an intentional ecosystem. This footer alone changes the perception of every individual node from *a single org's site* to *a node in something larger.*

##### Coordinated semantic metadata

On every published resource, an "ecosystem citation" panel — collapsed by default, expandable — shows: who else has linked to this resource within the network, what canonical entity this article reinforces, what shared ontology terms it uses, what languages it has been translated into across the network. Readers see this; AI crawlers see this; journalists checking authority see this.

#### On the dashboard side, for org leaders and Movemental operators

##### The Network Map

A live visualization of the five-node graph showing edge density between nodes — how many cross-citations, co-authored pieces, shared topics, and shared events each pair of nodes has produced in the last 90 days. The map highlights *underconnected pairs* — for example, if the theological voice and the church-mobilization node have produced fewer joint resources than the model expects, the dashboard flags it as a *connection opportunity* and suggests specific collaborations.

##### The Authority Convergence View

A panel showing which topics the network is gaining or losing semantic authority on, ranked.

> *Foster-care theology — convergence rising, driven by 14 new cross-cited pieces in Q1.*
>
> *Trauma-informed pastoral care — convergence stable, gap: practitioner content underweight.*

Each row links to the contributing articles across the network and shows where additional content from which node would compound authority fastest.

##### Shared Ontology Editor

A view where authorized editors at the hub propose new canonical terms; peer nodes see the proposal, can comment, can ratify or push back. When a term is ratified, it propagates across all five sites' tooltip definitions automatically within hours. Editors see the propagation status in real time — *defined by CAFO, ratified by 4 of 4 peers, live across 4,212 mentions network-wide.*

##### Cross-Node Editorial Calendar

A view where each node sees not just its own publishing schedule but the other four nodes' upcoming pieces, with the ability to coordinate — *the theological voice is publishing on family preservation Tuesday; the practitioner can land a complementary training piece Thursday; the church-mobilization node can drop a sermon kit Sunday.* What used to be four uncoordinated content calendars is one orchestrated rollout.

##### Movement-Wide Author Directory

A dashboard showing every named contributor across all five nodes — their home org, their cross-published work, their topical authority signal, their available collaboration capacity. A practitioner looking for a theological co-author for a piece on family preservation can filter the directory by topic, see who has bandwidth, and initiate the collaboration inside the dashboard.

##### Cross-Node Resource Library

Every PDF, sermon kit, training module, theological essay, family story, and dataset across the network is indexed in one library. Filterable by node, topic, format, language, audience. When a pastor at a partner church needs *trauma-informed care resources for small groups*, the library returns the canonical hub definition, two theological framings, three practitioner training modules, four sermon kits, and five family stories — drawn from all five nodes, surfaced as one result set.

##### Network Health Indicators

A row of indicators showing:

- Cross-node citation density (rising / stable / falling)
- Shared ontology adoption rate (% of articles using the canonical glossary)
- Peer-node link reciprocity
- Topic coverage gaps (where the model expects coverage but none exists)
- Translation parity (which languages each node is covered in)
- Event coordination signal

These are not analytics in the conventional sense. They are *structural integrity readings* of the network as a network.

##### The Discernment Memo View

Inherited from the Movemental Sandbox methodology — a per-node and network-wide view of which AI use cases the ecosystem has adjudicated green, yellow, and red. A practitioner exploring whether to use AI for case-note drafting sees that the theological voice has flagged a related use case yellow, with disclosure requirements; the church-mobilization node has piloted a similar workflow and documented its findings. **Adjudication is shared rather than per-org, which means the network learns once and governs everywhere.**

##### Movement-Wide Translation Dashboard

As the Scenii grows beyond English, a view showing every published resource's translation status across the six target languages. Editors can see at a glance — *this CAFO statistics page is canonical in English, ratified in Spanish, machine-drafted in Portuguese awaiting human review, queued for Korean.* The ten-year translation move lives here, operationally.

##### Cross-Node Donor & Newsletter Coordination

Subscribers can opt in to ecosystem-level newsletters in addition to per-org newsletters. Donors who give to one org see — *with consent, never automatically* — the other four nodes' work that intersects with what they care about. Each org retains its donor list. **The network creates introduction surface area without exfiltration.**

##### Provenance and Authorship Logs

For each published piece across the network, a visible log showing the human author, the AI assistance used (if any) and at what level, the editors who reviewed it, the ratification status of the canonical terms it uses, and the cross-citations it carries. This is the Safety Guidebook's transparency framework operationalized network-wide. Provenance is visible to readers in collapsed form on every article and to operators in full form on the dashboard.

---

#### What this list demonstrates

Almost none of the above is achievable by an individual freelancer building five separate sites. The cross-node citation chrome requires shared schema. The unified search requires a federated index. The shared glossary tooltips require canonical ontology with ratification flow. The topic pages require coordinated taxonomy. The authority convergence view requires graph-level analytics across all five nodes' content. The provenance logs require shared standards.

None of these are features. They are *evidence that the ecosystem is structurally real.*

The single-node site cannot fake any of them, because each one requires the other four nodes to exist and to have agreed to the architecture.

**The ecosystem looks like a network that knows itself. The single node looks like a website that doesn't know what room it's in.**
