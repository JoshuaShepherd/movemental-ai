### What each content type actually does

*A mapping of content types to the four jobs, with weighted contribution and the execution conditions that unlock each.*
*Calibrated for Josh Shepherd — cold start, no published corpus, proof-first. Brad's weights differ and are noted at the end.*

---

#### Before the table

Two things about the scores.

**They are judgment, not measurement.** They come from how these types behave generally and from your specific position, not from data about your site, which does not exist yet. Treat them as a starting argument to disagree with.

**The same type scores completely differently depending on execution.** An article built as a one-off scores 3 on Found. The same article built as part of a cluster answering a question people actually ask scores 9. This is the important part of the document, and it is in the sections after the table. The numbers assume the execution conditions are met.

Scores are out of 10, per job, independently. They do not sum to anything.

#### Found

*Someone who does not know your name encounters you.*

**Top contributors:** cluster articles (9), podcast guesting (9), co-bylines (8).

##### What unlocks it

**Cluster articles only work as clusters.** A single excellent article on a topic is close to invisible. Six to eight interlinked pieces around one pillar, each answering a distinct real question, all linking to a definitional hub, is what both search and retrieval systems resolve to. The unit of discovery is the cluster, not the piece. If you can only commit to three articles, pick a narrower pillar rather than writing three orphans.

The second condition: the article has to answer a question in the form it gets asked. "What is an AI sandbox and how does a church run one" is findable. "Reflections on discernment in a technological age" is not, however good it is.

Third: it needs a self-contained answer near the top that could be lifted whole. That is what gets quoted by a machine, and being quoted is the new front door.

**Podcast guesting only works with transcripts and warm hosts.** Prioritize shows that post text. An untranscribed episode is reach without durability. And accept everything in your network's orbit before pursuing cold pitches, because the conversion difference is enormous.

**Co-bylines work because of the domain, not the name.** A piece under both your name and Alan's, published on a site that already has authority, does something no amount of your own publishing can do for a year. This is the single highest-leverage Found move available to you and it costs a conversation.

**What does not get you found:** your story page, your work index, your newsletter. All valuable, none discoverable. Do not confuse effort with reach.

#### Kept

*Attention becomes a channel you own.*

**Top contributors:** newsletter (10), free diagnostic tool (9), email welcome flow (8).

##### What unlocks it

**The newsletter has to be worth opening on its own,** not a notification that you published something. The failure mode is the digest, and it is nearly universal. A newsletter that says "here is what I wrote this week" gets skimmed and unsubscribed. One that contains a thought not published anywhere else gets read and replied to.

Cadence matters less than consistency. Every other week, reliably, beats weekly for two months and then silence.

**The diagnostic tool has to give the reader something about themselves.** This is the whole distinction. A PDF about your framework is about you. A readiness check that tells a leader where their organization actually stands is about them, and it is the reason someone gives you an email address they will actually open.

It also has to produce a result the person would forward to a colleague. That is the test.

**The welcome flow only works if it delivers rather than nurtures.** Three or four emails. The first gives them the thing immediately. The second is the origin, short. The third points at one specific piece of work. The fourth asks a question and invites a reply. Nothing that reads as a sequence.

**What does not keep anyone:** anything that requires them to remember to come back.

#### Proven

*Evidence you are worth the time. Your bottleneck.*

**Top contributors:** how-I-built-it (10), book (10), case study (9), screen capture (9), academic piece (9).

##### What unlocks it

**How-I-built-it proves nothing without the wrinkle.** A build that went smoothly is a brochure. The piece establishes credibility through the part where something broke, you were wrong, and you say what it cost. Everyone has read the smooth version and nobody believes it. This is the single most common way this form gets ruined, and it gets ruined by the author trying to look competent.

Second condition: one checkable result. A link someone can open, a number with a date. "It works well" is not proof and reads as its opposite.

**Screen capture proves at a ratio nothing else matches.** Sixty to ninety seconds of the actual thing running, embedded in the piece, costs you almost nothing and cannot be faked. For a builder this is the cheapest credibility on the entire list, and you are underusing it badly.

**Case studies require permission and specifics.** An anonymized case study proves very little. If Youthfront will let you name them, with numbers, that one document does more for Proven than five essays. Ask early, before the work is done, when it is easy to say yes.

**The academic piece is slow and durable.** One properly cited article on authorship and machine mediation, in a real venue, changes how retrieval systems treat you permanently. It is the only item on this list whose value increases every year. Not urgent, and worth starting.

**A book scores 10 and I am not recommending one.** It is the strongest proof artifact that exists and it is eighteen months you do not have. Noted so the ranking is honest, not as advice.

**What proves nothing:** claims about capability, credentials without artifacts, and any sentence containing the word "expertise."

#### Moved

*They do the thing.*

**Top contributors:** welcome flow (9), diagnostic tool (8), course (8), case study (7).

##### What unlocks it

**One ask per piece.** A piece pointing at three things points at nothing. The article ends with the same single next step every time, and the step is small.

**The tool moves because it creates a qualified conversation.** Someone who has completed a readiness check and seen their own result is in a different position from someone who liked an essay. They have a finding about themselves and a question. That is the shortest path from reader to conversation in the whole system.

**The case study moves because it removes the imagination burden.** A prospect does not have to picture what working with you is like. Pair it with the tool and the sequence is nearly complete: here is where you stand, here is what happened when someone in your position acted.

**The course moves but should not be built yet.** You do not have the audience to tell you what it should contain. It scores well and belongs in year two.

**What does not move anyone:** the newsletter alone, essays with soft closes, anything ending in "reach out if you'd like to chat."

---

#### What this implies about sequence

Read the columns rather than the rows and the order becomes obvious.

Your Proven column is nearly empty and it is the column that gates the others. A stranger who finds you and is not convinced does not subscribe, and a subscriber who is not convinced does not act. So the first three or four pieces are how-I-built-it with screen captures, plus the story page, plus the work index. That is the proof block and it is roughly a month.

Kept is cheap and immediate. The newsletter and the readiness check exist or nearly exist. Open them at the same time as the proof block so nothing leaks.

Found is the slow one and it starts the day the proof block is done, because a cluster takes months to compound and there is no way to accelerate it. Podcast guesting and co-bylines run in parallel from day one, because they borrow authority instead of building it.

Moved is a wiring problem, not a content problem. One ask, one flow, one destination.

#### How this changes for Brad

Worth including because the contrast shows what the weights are actually made of.

Brad's Proven column is already full. Six books, a coined term, thirty years. Every proof-heavy type drops sharply in value for him: how-I-built-it goes from 10 to about 4, the work index becomes redundant, the academic piece is unnecessary.

His deficits are Found and Kept. He is known in rooms and invisible to the index, and his audience is warm but unowned. So his weights invert: cluster articles rise toward the top of the list, the tool becomes his front door because his audience is practitioners who want something to run, and network placements are trivially available to him in a way they are not to you.

Same platform, same type menu, opposite order.

**The general rule:** find your emptiest column, then build against it first. Publishing into a column you have already filled feels productive and changes nothing.
