Your institution needs more than an AI policy. It needs a way to respond together.

Want to know what is actually happening first? Take the Reality Assessment

One institution. Many different responses.

  • A faculty member permits AI-assisted writing; another prohibits it.
  • Administrative teams use incompatible AI services.
  • Academic leadership establishes general principles, but departments lack implementation guidance.
  • A denominational network tries to support churches operating in very different contexts.

The central issue is not merely inconsistent technology. It is the relationship between institutional authority and local responsibility.

An institution that answers once, for everyone, will be ignored where it doesn’t fit. One that leaves every unit alone will fragment. The work is deciding what must be shared and what must stay local.

Two ways to begin

Individual conviction

Students, faculty, staff and leaders can each write a Resolution about what they believe the institution should do. Writing one carries no institutional authority, whatever your position.

Write your Resolution

Institutional reality

Authorized leaders use the Reality Assessment to see the difference between official expectations and what people actually do. Participation is voluntary, answers are anonymous, and nothing appears until at least five people have answered.

Begin the Reality Assessment

A Resolution is a person’s conviction, not institutional policy. Results are counted only for the people an institution invites, and only as totals.

The Map at institutional scale

The same five stages, carried out at two levels: commitments the whole institution shares, and decisions that stay with each department, campus or affiliate.

  1. 1Decide what you will stand for

    Develop institution-wide principles with explicit local decision rights, so every unit knows what it may decide for itself.

  2. 2Gather what should ground the work

    Identify source repositories, rights, permissions and workflows, and which knowledge is shared and which is local.

  3. 3Practice the judgment your people will need

    Provide role-specific formation for leaders, faculty, staff and affiliates, led by people inside each unit.

  4. 4Build only what belongs

    Choose shared infrastructure and local applications according to actual needs, not one deployment for everyone.

THE NETWORK MODEL

One foundation. Many responsible organizations.

A network can offer the response to every member at once, without turning shared infrastructure into shared data.

  • Shared onboarding and common resources.
  • Local governance and organization-specific context.
  • Shared technical infrastructure where it fits.
  • Separate permissions and approved data boundaries for every member.
  • Optional specialized tools for particular affiliates.
  • Central support without unrestricted central access to any member’s private information.

A conceptual demonstration, not specific technical or contractual terms. How the network offer is structured is still being decided.
